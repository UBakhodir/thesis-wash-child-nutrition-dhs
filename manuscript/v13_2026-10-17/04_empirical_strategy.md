# 4. Empirical strategy

Throughout, $i$ indexes a child, $h$ the child's household, $c$ the child's DHS cluster, $r$ the child's administrative region (each cluster belongs to exactly one region), and $s$ a survey round (country). All specifications below are estimated and reported in this thesis as **associational**, not causal; none should be read, described, or cited elsewhere in this thesis as identifying a causal effect. None of the designs below is a panel design, a difference-in-differences design, or an instrumental-variables design. These terms are not used anywhere in this thesis to describe a specification that is not what is actually estimated.

## 4.1 Baseline: household WASH, region fixed effects

$$Y_{ihcr} = \alpha + \beta_W\,\text{Water}_{ih} + \beta_S\,\text{Sanitation}_{ih} + X_i'\gamma + \delta_r + \varepsilon_{ihcr}$$

$\text{Water}_{ih}$ and $\text{Sanitation}_{ih}$ are the child's own household water and sanitation status, **entered together in the same model** (every household-WASH specification in this thesis estimates $\beta_W$ and $\beta_S$ jointly, never one at a time). $X_i$ is the covariate set of Section 3.4, and $\delta_r$ a fixed effect for each of the 114 pooled administrative regions, so $\beta_W$ and $\beta_S$ are identified from **within-region** variation only, net of controls and of the other WASH measure. A region can contain many DHS clusters, so $\delta_r$ still allows clusters within it to differ in local infrastructure, environment, or health-service access, plausibly correlated with both household WASH and child nutrition and not absorbed here, motivating the cluster-fixed-effects specification below.

## 4.2 Baseline: household WASH, cluster fixed effects (main specification)

$$Y_{ihc} = \alpha_c + \beta_W\,\text{Water}_{ih} + \beta_S\,\text{Sanitation}_{ih} + X_i'\gamma + \varepsilon_{ihc}$$

As in Section 4.1, $\text{Water}_{ih}$ and $\text{Sanitation}_{ih}$ are entered together, mutually adjusted; $\alpha_c$, a separate fixed effect for each DHS cluster, identifies $\beta_W$ and $\beta_S$ from differences in household WASH status *among households within the same cluster*, conditional on the other covariates. It absorbs anything every household in a cluster shares (local infrastructure, environment, market access) but not differences between households within it, which can still differ in wealth, education, or unobserved behaviour connected to both WASH status and growth, keeping this specification associational. One mechanical consequence: since every DHS cluster is entirely urban or rural by design, urban/rural residence is absorbed by the fixed effect and cannot be estimated separately. **Wealth quintile is included in $X_i$ and estimated, not omitted**: fixed effects absorb only what is constant within a cluster, and wealth varies within clusters just as WASH status does (31.6% of clusters vary in own-water status, 50.6% in own-sanitation, the variation identifying $\beta_W$/$\beta_S$; a comparable check confirms wealth varies too), so there is no mechanism to remove it, and none was observed.

## 4.3 Baseline: community WASH, region fixed effects

$$Y_{ihcr} = \alpha + \beta_W\,\bar{C}^{\,water}_{-h,c} + \beta_S\,\bar{C}^{\,san}_{-h,c} + X_i'\gamma + \delta_r + \varepsilon_{ihcr}$$

$\bar{C}^{\,water}_{-h,c}$ and $\bar{C}^{\,san}_{-h,c}$ are the leave-one-out community water and sanitation coverage rates defined in Section 3.3. They are again **entered together and mutually adjusted**, including in the "minimally adjusted" stage of Table 5.2, which still adjusts each community measure for the other even though it adjusts for nothing else. These exposures are, by construction, properties of the cluster rather than of the individual child. Here $\beta_W$ and $\beta_S$ are therefore identified mainly by comparing *clusters* with different community coverage within the same administrative region, net of the same controls. As with the household region-fixed-effects model, unobserved differences between clusters inside the same region are not removed. This is an associational estimate of a contextual exposure, not an estimate of a causal spillover effect.

**Why cluster fixed effects are not used for this exposure.** Community WASH could, in principle, be estimated with cluster fixed effects, but the estimate would not be informative: the leave-one-out rate is itself a cluster-level quantity, and within a cluster each child's value differs from every other's only because a different household is excluded from each calculation — a mechanical artefact. Measured directly, the within-cluster standard deviation of this rate is approximately 0.007 (water) and 0.011 (sanitation), against an overall standard deviation of approximately 0.35 for both, so a cluster fixed effect would absorb nearly all genuine between-cluster variation, identifying the coefficient almost entirely from this small mechanical residual rather than real differences in community environments. The historical extension (Section 4.6) differs fundamentally: a real time dimension (birth-cohort variation within cluster) gives its cluster fixed effects genuine, non-mechanical variation to work with. Cluster fixed effects are therefore appropriate for household and historical WASH but not current-round community WASH — a design consequence of the leave-one-out construction, not an inconsistency.

## 4.4 Baseline: household and community WASH, jointly

$$Y_{ihcr} = \alpha + \beta_{HW}\,\text{Water}_{ih} + \beta_{HS}\,\text{Sanitation}_{ih} + \beta_{CW}\,\bar{C}^{\,water}_{-h,c} + \beta_{CS}\,\bar{C}^{\,san}_{-h,c} + X_i'\gamma + \delta_r + \varepsilon_{ihcr}$$

All four WASH terms (household water, household sanitation, community water, community sanitation) are entered **together, in one model**, and each coefficient is conditional on the other three. $\beta_{HW}$/$\beta_{HS}$ are the household associations conditional on surrounding community coverage and on each other; $\beta_{CW}$/$\beta_{CS}$ are the community associations conditional on the household's own status and on each other. Because household and community water measures are fairly closely correlated, this specification is reported as a secondary decomposition, not a preferred model.

$$
\begin{aligned}
Y_i = {}& \alpha + \beta_W\,\text{Water}_i + \beta_S\,\text{Sanitation}_i + \sum_{a \neq \text{ref}} \theta_a\,\text{AgeBand}_{ia} \\
&+ \sum_{a \neq \text{ref}} \lambda^W_a\,(\text{Water}_i \times \text{AgeBand}_{ia}) + \sum_{a \neq \text{ref}} \lambda^S_a\,(\text{Sanitation}_i \times \text{AgeBand}_{ia}) \\
&+ X_i'\gamma + \text{FE} + \varepsilon_i
\end{aligned}
$$

Water and sanitation, and both of their full interaction blocks, are entered **together in one model**, exactly as in every other household specification in this thesis. $\text{AgeBand}_{ia}$ are six current-age bands in completed months (0–5, 6–11, 12–23, 24–35, 36–47, 48–59). They replace the continuous age control used elsewhere, with **12–23 months as the reference band**, fixed before any heterogeneity result was examined. Each of the other five bands therefore contributes one $\theta_a$ main-effect dummy and one interaction term per exposure ($\lambda^W_a$, $\lambda^S_a$), ten interaction terms in total. Three joint Wald tests are reported per outcome and fixed-effect structure: a water-specific test ($H_0: \lambda^W_a = 0 \ \forall a$, 5 restrictions), a sanitation-specific test (5 restrictions), and a combined test of all ten interaction terms jointly (10 restrictions). Each is referred to a $\chi^2$ distribution with its stated degrees of freedom, not an $F$ distribution. The $\lambda^W_a$/$\lambda^S_a$ terms test whether the survey-date WASH association differs across children's current ages (Section 1.2); this is not a reconstruction of what WASH conditions an older child actually experienced in infancy, and is kept distinct from the historical extension's exposure-timing design throughout.

## 4.5 Inference, weighting, and the pooled estimand

**Weighting.** For a model with an estimation sample of size $N$ pooled across $M$ countries, every country is given equal total weight in the pooled regression. Both pipelines implement this with each child's weight computed as
$$w_i = \text{raw}_i \big/ \sum_{j \in \text{country}(i)} \text{raw}_j$$
so that each country's weights sum to exactly $1$. Here $\text{raw}_i$ is the sample's own native sampling-weight field (`v005`-derived for the baseline, Section 3.4; `PERWEIGHT` for the historical extension, Section 3.8), computed within that model's own final estimation sample after all applicable exclusions (including the historical pipeline's singleton-cluster removal, see below; the baseline pipeline applies none). The two pipelines' conventions are substantively identical (equal total country weight); the historical pipeline additionally multiplies by a constant that only rescales reporting and has no effect on any coefficient, standard error, confidence interval, or p-value (Appendix B.1 gives the proof). ($M$ denotes the number of countries, deliberately a different symbol from $K$, the regressor count used below.) **This equal-country-total weighting is an authorised, disclosed working choice, not a correction for a sampling design, and it does not, by itself, define the pooled coefficient as any particular simple average.**

The actual estimand is the single common slope $\beta$ of one pooled weighted regression, run jointly across all countries' observations at once — not the result of a two-step procedure that estimates each country's own slope separately and averages them. Equal country weight *totals* do **not** make the pooled coefficient the arithmetic mean of the country-specific coefficients: by Frisch–Waugh–Lovell logic, a country's effective influence on the pooled slope depends jointly on its weights and its residual exposure variation after controls, so a country with little residual within-cluster variation contributes little statistical information even at an equal weight total. This thesis's own historical pooled water–HAZ estimate illustrates the point: the simple unweighted average of the three established-date countries' own coefficients is 0.58, while the actual pooled coefficient is 1.03 (Section 5.6), consistent with Kenya's residual exposure variation being roughly a third of Nigeria's and under half of Ghana's, so Kenya's imprecise, negative estimate receives comparatively little weight despite its equal weight total. For the primary historical established-date estimates, each pooled coefficient is reported alongside its country-specific components (Tables 5.5–5.6) and residual exposure variation (Section 4.6); the baseline pooled models (Sections 5.1–5.3) are not broken out by country in the same way.

**Estimator and inference, stated separately for each pipeline because they are not identical.** Household- and historical-model cluster fixed effects are both implemented as weighted within-cluster demeaning, exactly equivalent to weighted least squares with a full set of cluster dummy variables, using the cluster-robust CRV1 sandwich estimator with the small-sample correction $c = \frac{G}{G-1}\times\frac{N-1}{N-K}$ ($G$ = clusters, $K$ = non-fixed-effect regressors retained — the project's adjudicated convention when the fixed-effect and clustering groupings coincide). **The two pipelines differ, however, in the reference distribution used for confidence intervals and p-values.** The historical pipeline uses the Student *t*-distribution with $G-1$ degrees of freedom; the baseline pipeline (`statsmodels` [@Seabold2010Statsmodels]) does **not** — its run log records `use_t: False`, confirmed to match a normal (z) reference distribution to more than ten decimal places (Appendix B.1). The region-fixed-effects models (Sections 4.1, 4.3, 4.4) are weighted least squares with explicit region dummies clustered on `country_psu`, counting region dummies and the constant in $K$, and likewise use the normal reference distribution. This thesis's primary baseline household cluster-FE p-values (water HAZ .062, sanitation HAZ .548) are therefore normal-reference values; a more conservative convention that additionally penalises degrees of freedom for the absorbed cluster dummies gives wider sensitivity p-values (.080, .574), reported as such wherever cited, never silently substituted (Appendix B.1).

**Singleton-cluster treatment, stated separately for each pipeline.** Before estimation, the historical pipeline explicitly drops clusters that contribute only one observation to a given model's final estimation sample and reports the number dropped with every model, since a singleton cluster carries no within-cluster information and cannot identify a cluster-fixed-effects coefficient. **The baseline pipeline's household- and community-model code contains no corresponding step.** This thesis therefore neither asserts that baseline singletons are dropped nor reports a baseline singleton count, since no such step exists in the code that produced the baseline tables in Section 5 — a disclosed description gap, not an assumption that it is numerically inconsequential. Sample size, cluster count, the finite-sample correction $c$ above, and the country-weight normalisation (Section 4.5) could all be affected, and this thesis has not quantified that effect for the baseline models.

**Rank and identification are computational properties, not causal ones.** Every reported model's design matrix is checked for full column rank after the fixed-effects transformation. The historical pipeline drops any fully absorbed column and records it by name; the baseline pipeline stops if the design is rank-deficient, and its only pre-specified exclusion (`residence` under cluster fixed effects) is stated in Section 4.2. Neither pipeline silently reports an unstable coefficient: the stated equation is computationally well defined and its coefficient is the one the equation describes, but that does not establish a causal parameter, and this thesis does not conflate the two.

## 4.6 Historical extension: cluster fixed effects with cohort controls

$$y_{ic} = \alpha_c + \beta\,E_{ic}(w, p) + \lambda\,\text{age}_{ic} + \gamma'X_{ic} + \delta_{s,b_{ic}} + \varepsilon_{ic}$$

$E_{ic}(w,p)$ is the annual-weighted IHME mean (product $p$, window $w$) assigned to child $i$ in cluster $c$, on the 0–100 percentage-point scale (Sections 3.5–3.7); $\beta$ is reported per 1 and per 10 percentage points. $\alpha_c$ is the cluster fixed effect. $\delta_{s,b}$ is a survey-specific (country-specific) birth-year term: each country's own set of birth-year fixed effects, estimated relative to that country's own earliest observed birth year rather than to one pooled reference year across all three countries. An early independent-reconstruction attempt used a single pooled reference year for the birth-year terms and omitted the age-in-months control, together producing a materially different coefficient (1.385 vs. the correct 1.026). A dedicated diagnostic isolates the age-in-months omission as the sole cause: the two birth-year schemes are equivalent nuisance specifications for the exposure coefficient, confirmed to floating-point precision (Appendix B.2 gives the complete derivation). $X_{ic}$ includes child sex, maternal age, maternal education, and marital status. Wealth is excluded from the main specification for the reason given in Section 3.8, not because cluster fixed effects absorb it.

**Why cluster fixed effects are appropriate here but not for current-round community WASH (Section 4.3).** The historical exposure $E_{ic}(w,p)$ varies not only across clusters but also, genuinely, across birth cohorts *within* the same cluster: a child born in 2014 and one born in 2017 in the same cluster can have experienced different modelled local WASH coverage during their own early-life windows. This is real temporal variation, not the mechanical construction artefact that rules out cluster fixed effects for the current-round leave-one-out community measure, conditional on there being enough of it for the fixed effect not to absorb nearly all of it. That variation is quantified, per model, as the residual exposure standard deviation after all controls (Section 5.6), ranging from 0.20 to 0.88 percentage points depending on country and product — modest relative to the 10-percentage-point scale conventionally used to report the coefficient, which this thesis does not interpret as describing an exposure contrast typically observed within a cluster.

Weighting follows the same equal-country-total formula as Section 4.5, with $M=3$ countries for the established-date pooled models (Ghana, Kenya, Nigeria). Inference uses the Student *t*(*G*−1) reference distribution that the historical pipeline actually implements (Section 4.5 states precisely how this differs from the baseline pipeline's normal-reference inference). Model-specific samples are constructed independently for every window and product combination. Singleton clusters are dropped and counted by the historical pipeline's own explicit filtering step; this is a property of the historical pipeline's code specifically, not a claim that the baseline pipeline does the same (Section 4.5).
