# 1. Introduction

Child stunting remains one of the most persistent markers of population health disadvantage in Sub-Saharan Africa. Linear growth faltering concentrates in the first two years of life and is difficult to reverse once established, with consequences that extend well beyond anthropometry into cognitive development, school attainment, and adult earning capacity [@Victora2008; @Victora2010; @Black2013]. Among the environmental exposures proposed to explain why children in similar economic circumstances grow at such different rates, water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) conditions occupy a central place in both the public-health and development-economics literatures. The proposed mechanism is biologically direct: inadequate water and sanitation increase a child's exposure to faecal pathogens, raising the frequency of diarrhoeal and sub-clinical enteric infection, which in turn diverts nutritional intake toward immune response rather than growth — a pathway often described as environmental enteric dysfunction [@CummingCairncross2016]. This biological mechanism is well established and is not disputed even by the strongest recent causal evidence against basic WASH interventions reducing stunting (Section 2.3 discusses this distinction precisely). What is considerably less settled is whether the mechanism translates into a *detectable, policy-relevant statistical association* in observational survey data, once household wealth and local development are accounted for, and whether a *basic facility-level* WASH classification — as opposed to a comprehensive intervention package — is the right exposure to look for it in. This thesis is concerned with that narrower, empirical question.

## 1.1 Two distinct exposures: a household's own facilities and its surroundings

A household's own water source and sanitation facility are not the only WASH-related exposure a child faces. Even a child in a household with an improved latrine and piped water can be exposed to pathogens through shared water sources, open defecation by neighbours, flooding, or simply proximity to inadequately managed waste if the surrounding community lacks similar facilities. This distinction — between **own-household WASH**, whose protective benefit accrues most directly to the household's own members, and **surrounding-community WASH**, a local environmental condition shaped by every household's facilities together — is not a modelling convenience; it corresponds to two different exposure pathways. The distinction is not a claim that household WASH is a purely private good with no effect beyond its own household: improved household sanitation in particular is a textbook source of a local environmental externality, since it reduces the pathogen load the household itself releases into the shared local environment, benefiting neighbours as well. Recent community-level WASH research [@Donohue2023; @SkoufiasVinha2026] suggests household and community WASH need not move together or carry the same association with child growth, which is precisely why this thesis constructs and reports both rather than treating either as a complete description of a child's WASH exposure. This thesis constructs and compares both: a child's own household classification, and a leave-one-out measure of improved-facility coverage among every *other* household in the same DHS cluster, so that a child's own household is never used to construct its own community exposure.

## 1.2 Exposure timing in cross-sectional survey designs

A specific measurement limitation affects the cross-sectional, repeated-survey-round observational literature this thesis's baseline analysis belongs to, including DHS-based studies generally [@Rakotomanana2020; @Addae2024] and this thesis's own baseline: household and community WASH status is recorded once, at the survey date, and applied to every child in that round regardless of the child's age. A four-year-old measured in a 2022 survey experienced their nutritionally critical first two years of life around 2018–2019, under WASH conditions the survey-date measurement cannot describe, while an infant measured in the same round has WASH recorded very close to their own early life. This limitation is specific to designs of this kind; it does not describe the major WASH-intervention trials, which assign exposure near each child's own birth and follow growth prospectively over a defined window (Section 2.3), nor does it describe panel designs that track WASH and health indicators over successive rounds within the same geographic units (Section 2.2). Age controls and age-interaction tests, which this thesis also estimates, describe how the *current* survey-date association varies with how old a child is today; they do not reconstruct what a particular child's own environment actually was during their own developmental window. This thesis's main, baseline analysis documents this limitation explicitly and does not claim to resolve it.

A separate, clearly bounded extension responds to one specific dimension of this limitation: rather than asking whether assigning a *historical* exposure instead of a *current* one changes an otherwise-identical estimate — which this thesis's baseline and historical samples differ too much in other respects to isolate (Section 6.2 states this precisely) — the extension asks a self-contained question within its own sample: **is modelled local WASH coverage during a child's own early-life window associated with that child's later anthropometric outcomes?** It does so by matching each child's birth-cohort location to a multi-year, modelled local WASH coverage surface (IHME's gridded 2000–2017 drinking-water and sanitation access estimates [@LBDWaSH2020]) for a defined early-life window, which gives genuine within-cluster, cross-birth-cohort variation that a single survey round cannot offer. This is a measurement improvement on one specific dimension — assigning an exposure closer to each child's own developmental period, rather than to the survey date — not a resolution of the exposure-timing problem in general, and not a reconstruction of what any individual household's actual historical WASH facilities were, which no available data source for this thesis can provide. The extension's own, separate limitations (undocumented measurement-subsample weighting, DHS/IHME input overlap, residence used as a proxy for historical residence, confidentiality-driven coordinate displacement) are stated in full in Section 6 and govern how much weight its results can bear.

## 1.3 Research questions

This thesis asks three related questions. First, is a child's own household's water and sanitation status associated with their anthropometric outcomes (height-for-age, weight-for-age, weight-for-height), after accounting for household and cluster-level confounding? Second, is the WASH status of the surrounding community — independent of the child's own household — separately associated with these outcomes? Third, within the historical sample's own three countries, is modelled local WASH coverage during a child's specific early-life window associated with these outcomes? All three questions are posed and answered as **associations under a cross-sectional design**, not as estimates of causal effects; this is stated once here and governs how every coefficient in this thesis should be read. The third question is answered only within the historical sample; it is not compared to the first two questions' answers as if the comparison isolated the effect of exposure timing, for the reasons given in Section 1.2 and developed fully in Section 6.2.

## 1.4 Scope: four countries, two time horizons

The baseline analysis pools the most recent Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) round available, as of the data-collection date, for four countries: Ethiopia (2024–25), Ghana (2022), Kenya (2022), and Nigeria (2024). These four countries were selected for data availability and recency, not as a representative sample of Sub-Saharan Africa, and no claim in this thesis generalises findings from these four countries to the region as a whole. The historical extension uses an earlier DHS round for three of the four countries — Ghana (2014), Kenya (2014), and Nigeria (2018) — linked to IHME's annual gridded WASH-coverage estimates, which are available through 2017. Ethiopia's 2016 round is retained only as a separately labelled, provisional sensitivity analysis, because the Ethiopian-calendar interview-date conversion required to place Ethiopian children in a specific early-life calendar window could not be fully validated (Section 3.5 and Appendix C). The baseline and historical samples are **not the same children**, use different exposure definitions and spatial/temporal constructions, and are analysed, validated, and reported separately throughout; no result in one sample is used to adjust, select, or validate a result in the other.

## 1.5 Contribution

This thesis makes two separate contributions, in two separate samples, which should not be read as one combined design. In the **baseline sample**, the contribution is the explicit, side-by-side construction and comparison of own-household and leave-one-out community WASH exposure within one four-country sample and one consistent set of controls, each exposure estimated under the fixed-effects structure its own construction requires (Section 4.2–4.3). In the **historical sample**, the contribution is a transparently documented, preliminary attempt to introduce genuine early-life temporal exposure variation through geospatially matched historical coverage data; this extension examines a single modelled local-coverage exposure assigned to each child's own early-life window (Section 3.5–3.8) and does **not** reproduce the household-versus-community comparison — the historical data source does not distinguish a child's own household from its surrounding community, so no household/community contrast exists to draw within the historical sample. Having a particular combination of four countries is not, by itself, evidence of substantive novelty, and this thesis does not claim it as such; the specific design choices above are each reported with the methodological limitations of their own sample treated as integral to their interpretation rather than as closing caveats.

## 1.6 A restrained summary of findings

Stated briefly here and developed with full uncertainty in Sections 5–6: household-level WASH shows no statistically significant association with height-for-age once cluster fixed effects are used to compare households within the same community (water: β = −0.085, 95% CI [−0.175, 0.004], *p* = .062; sanitation: β = 0.026, 95% CI [−0.058, 0.109], *p* = .548). Community-level WASH coverage shows a large, strongly significant unadjusted association that attenuates to statistical insignificance once socioeconomic controls and region fixed effects are added (water: 0.447 unadjusted vs. 0.033 adjusted, *p* = .573; sanitation: 0.472 unadjusted vs. 0.008 adjusted, *p* = .894) — a pattern consistent with, but not conclusive proof of, confounding by local socioeconomic conditions. Within the historical sample, the pooled established-date water–HAZ association is positive and significant (β per 10 percentage points of modelled coverage = 1.03, 95% CI [0.51, 1.54], *p* < .001) but is sensitive to the weighting convention used (falling to 0.41 unweighted on the same observations) and carries a substantial list of unresolved documentation issues (Section 6; Appendix C). None of these results should be read as evidence either for or against the effectiveness of WASH investment as a policy instrument; they describe statistical associations in specific survey samples, nothing more.

## 1.7 Design and principal limitations, stated at the outset

This is a cross-sectional, associational design throughout. It is not a panel, not a difference-in-differences design, and not an instrumental-variables design, and none of those terms is used elsewhere in this thesis to describe a method that is not actually implemented. The principal limitations — unresolved sampling-weight documentation for the historical extension's measured-anthropometry subsample, DHS/IHME input overlap in two of the three historical-extension countries, the use of current residence as a proxy for historical residence, confidentiality-driven displacement of DHS cluster coordinates, and the small number of countries in both samples — are stated in full in Section 6 and are not treated there as a closing formality.

## 1.8 Structure

Section 2 situates this thesis within the WASH–child-growth literature, distinguishing biological plausibility from the specific question of whether a facility-level classification shows a detectable association, and distinguishing causal evidence from conditional association. Section 3 describes the data, sample construction, and variable definitions for both the baseline and historical samples. Section 4 sets out the empirical strategy, with notation matched to the actual estimating equations. Section 5 reports results. Section 6 discusses findings in relation to the literature, states in detail why the baseline and historical results are not directly comparable as a test of exposure timing, and states all limitations in full.
