# 6. Discussion and limitations

## 6.1 Relating the findings to the literature

This thesis's baseline findings sit consistently alongside the existing literature, though the comparison is precise rather than general. Headey and Palloni [-@HeadeyPalloni2019], using a subnational-region panel across 59 countries with difference-in-differences regressions that net out region-level, time-invariant confounding, find that sanitation improvements predict large reductions in diarrhoea and child mortality but are **not** associated with changes in stunting or wasting once this within-region design is used, while improved water access is statistically insignificantly associated with most outcomes (piped water specifically is an exception, predicting reduced stunting). The null sanitation–stunting finding in their panel design is a close, specific parallel to this thesis's own null household- and community-sanitation associations with HAZ (Section 5.1–5.2): both studies find that a sanitation–stunting association that is detectable in less-controlled specifications is not detectable once a design better suited to netting out confounding is used, each in its own, different way (region-level panel fixed effects there; household- or cluster-level fixed effects here). This is a narrower and more defensible claim than saying WASH–growth associations "generally" attenuate under better controls, which overstates what any one study, including this thesis's own, establishes on its own.

The non-significant household cluster-fixed-effects associations in this thesis (water–HAZ *p* = .062; sanitation–HAZ *p* = .548) are also broadly consistent with the strongest available randomised evidence: the water, sanitation, hygiene, and combined-WASH arms of the WASH-Benefits Bangladesh, WASH-Benefits Kenya, and SHINE trials each showed no statistically detectable effect on linear growth in any of the three sites, despite high measured compliance — while, in the same trials, the separate nutrition-only arm (with no WASH component) showed a modest positive effect (Section 2.3) [@Cumming2019]. This consistency should not be overstated into confirmation, for a reason stated precisely in Section 2.3: this thesis's exposure is a facility-type classification in cross-sectional survey data, not a randomised intervention with prospective follow-up from near each child's birth, so the two literatures answer related but not identical questions. The one statistically significant household-level result in this thesis — household water conditional on community water, HAZ, negative and borderline significant (Section 5.3) — is a single cell among twelve estimated in that table and is not treated as evidence that improved household water is harmful to child growth; no mechanism in the literature reviewed in Section 2 supports that reading, and this thesis does not offer one.

The historical extension's positive, significant pooled water–HAZ association (1.03 per 10 points, *p* < .001) is the one result across both the baseline and historical analyses unlikely to be a multiplicity artefact of the many cells estimated (Section 5.6). It would be tempting to read this as evidence that introducing a time dimension recovers an association the single-round baseline design cannot detect. Section 6.2 explains in detail why this thesis does not draw that conclusion.

## 6.2 Why the baseline and historical results cannot be compared as a test of exposure timing

This is stated as its own subsection because it is the single most tempting overinterpretation this thesis's own structure invites, and because the historical extension's own research question (Section 1.2–1.3) is deliberately framed to avoid it: the historical extension asks whether modelled early-life local WASH coverage is associated with outcomes *within its own sample*, not whether assigning a historical rather than a current exposure changes an otherwise-identical estimate.

The baseline and historical samples differ along at least four dimensions simultaneously, not one:

1. **Different children and survey rounds.** The baseline uses 2022–2025 DHS rounds across four countries; the historical extension uses 2014–2018 rounds across three (Ethiopia excluded; Section 1.4). No child appears in both samples.
2. **Different exposure definitions and construction.** The baseline's household and community exposures are built from DHS household-census survey responses at the survey date (Section 3.3). The historical exposure is a modelled, area-averaged, annual gridded coverage estimate from an external source (IHME), matched to a specific postnatal window (Section 3.4–3.7). These are not the same quantity measured at two different times; they are two different kinds of measurement.
3. **Different eligibility and completion rules.** The historical sample additionally requires complete spatial coverage and a complete, interview-date-verified exposure window (Section 3.7), restrictions that have no baseline-sample counterpart.
4. **Different control sets and identifying variation.** The baseline's main household specification uses cluster fixed effects with region-level socioeconomic controls varying by specification; the historical specification uses cluster fixed effects with country-by-birth-year terms and excludes wealth from the main specification for a reason specific to the historical design (Section 3.8, Section 4.6). The historical cluster-fixed-effects model is identified by genuine within-cluster, cross-birth-cohort variation (Section 4.6); the baseline's community model is deliberately *not* estimated with cluster fixed effects, for a reason specific to its own leave-one-out construction (Section 4.3) that has no bearing on the historical design.

Because all four of these differ at once, a difference between the baseline's largely null associations and the historical extension's one significant association cannot be cleanly attributed to "the effect of assigning historical instead of current exposure." It could instead, or in addition, reflect different confounding structures across the two country sets, different measurement error properties of a modelled surface versus a survey response, the different control sets, or chance, given the number of cells estimated in each sample (Section 5.6's multiplicity point applies here too). This thesis does not attempt to adjudicate between these possibilities, because its design — two separately constructed samples, not a shared sample with only the exposure-timing dimension varied — cannot do so.

**What the within-historical-sample window comparison does and does not hold constant.** Section 5.7 compares four exposure windows (birth-year, prenatal, post-12-month, post-24-month) within the *same* historical sample's country set and estimation approach. This comparison holds constant the children's survey rounds, the exposure source and spatial-extraction method, the control set, and the estimator; it varies only which months of the IHME surface are averaged into each child's exposure value. It does **not** hold constant the estimation sample across all four windows (each window has its own eligible sample, differing by which children have complete coverage for that specific window; Section 5.7), and it does not speak at all to the baseline-versus-historical comparison discussed above, which this paragraph is careful to keep separate.

## 6.3 Limitations: unresolved documentation

Two issues in this thesis's historical extension remain genuinely unresolved documentation gaps, not design choices or inherent limitations. First, no DHS or IPUMS source could be located that documents a measurement-subsample or anthropometry-specific sampling weight, despite repeated attempts at the DHS Biomarker Manual and alternative official sources (Appendix A records the exact sources tried); this thesis's working inference — that within-survey PERWEIGHT normalisation removes a constant measurement-subsample selection factor under an assumption of random household-level selection — is stated as an inference, not a documented fact, and cannot correct for differential non-response among children who were eligible but not measured. Second, the gap between Nigeria 2018's official measured-child count (12,806) and the IPUMS birth-history extract's non-missing anthropometry count (11,704) remains unexplained. Consistent with both points, this thesis reports its historical coefficients as **weighted conditional associations among eligible measured children**, not as population-representative estimates for any defined population — and the use of an unweighted estimate alongside the weighted one (Section 5.6) does not resolve this concern, since the selection question is about who was measured at all, not about how measured children are weighted relative to each other.

## 6.4 Limitations: authorised design choices, disclosed and tested

Three features of this thesis's design are deliberate, disclosed choices, not errors. First, the equal-country-total pooled weighting (Section 4.5) targets an average-country association with each established-date country given equal weight, not a population-size-weighted estimate; its consequence — the pooled water–HAZ coefficient moves from 1.03 (weighted) to 0.41 (unweighted, same observations) — is disclosed and reported alongside the main estimate in every instance. Second, the working 12-month postnatal exposure window was chosen during the design phase, before any model was estimated, for a documented reason (an early-life window whose completion can be checked against the interview date); it is not described anywhere in this thesis as "preregistered," and the literature basis for this specific window length is modest — Cumming and Cairncross [-@CummingCairncross2016] support early-life exposure generally and, if anything, lean toward a 24-month window — which is why all four candidate windows (Section 5.7) are reported together. Third, wealth quintile is excluded from the historical extension's main specification because it may be a post-exposure mediator of historical WASH conditions and because it is correlated with the WASH exposure itself (Section 3.8), a substantive identification concern, not a claim that cluster fixed effects remove wealth mechanically, which this thesis's own baseline model demonstrates is false by including and estimating wealth directly (Section 4.2).

## 6.5 Limitations: inherent, requiring disclosure rather than resolution

DHS/IHME input overlap affects two of the three established-date historical countries (Ghana and Kenya), whose own DHS surveys feed into the IHME gridded estimates used as the exposure; this thesis reports the overlap-exclusion sensitivity (0.74 for the Ghana-excluded pooled estimate; Section 5.8) alongside the main estimate rather than treating the overlap as removable. Current residence, as recorded at the survey date, is used as a proxy for historical residence during a child's early-life window; any child who migrated between their early-life window and the survey date is assigned the wrong location's exposure, under the primary buffer-average method and under the containing-pixel sensitivity alike. DHS cluster coordinates are randomly displaced for confidentiality, and this limitation holds regardless of extraction method: an area-weighted buffer average (this thesis's primary method) reads pixels around the displaced coordinates, not the true ones, and does not recover the true location, does not validate or correct for the (undisclosed, per-cluster) displacement distribution DHS actually applies, and is not a population-weighted estimate (Section 3.4, Section 3.6); the containing-pixel sensitivity (Section 5.8) reads a single displaced pixel instead, which is subject to the same location uncertainty by a different mechanism — neither method is immune to it, and neither resolves it. This thesis's own spatial extraction, under its primary method, computes an area-weighted average of the raster surface within each buffer, which is a different quantity from the source study's own population-weighted administrative aggregate (Section 3.4). IHME's exposure surface is itself a *modelled* estimate with its own uncertainty, not a direct observation, and its reported upper/lower confidence bounds are not used as regression inputs. The surface is available only at annual resolution. The residual within-cluster exposure variation that actually identifies the historical coefficients (0.20–0.88 percentage points depending on country and product) is modest relative to the 10-point scale used for reporting (Section 5.6). Finally, Ethiopia's interview-date uncertainty (Section 5.9; Appendix C) is treated as an inherent limitation of that round's recoded date fields, not a design choice this thesis made.

## 6.6 What this thesis does not claim

This thesis does not claim that its findings establish a causal effect of WASH facilities on child growth, in either direction. It does not claim that its null or weak baseline associations are evidence that WASH investment is an ineffective use of development resources. It does not generalise findings from Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya, and Nigeria to Sub-Saharan Africa as a region. It does not treat the historical extension's computational verification (Appendix B) as evidence of improved causal identification. And, as developed fully in Section 6.2, it does not treat the difference between the baseline's largely null results and the historical extension's one significant result as evidence that exposure timing, specifically, explains that difference, because the two samples differ in several other respects that this thesis's design cannot separate from timing.
