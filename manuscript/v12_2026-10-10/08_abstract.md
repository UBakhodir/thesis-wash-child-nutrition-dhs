# Abstract

This thesis examines associations between household and community water and sanitation conditions and child anthropometric outcomes (height-for-age, weight-for-age, weight-for-height) using Demographic and Health Survey data from four Sub-Saharan African countries: Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya, and Nigeria. It distinguishes throughout between a child's own household facilities and a leave-one-out measure of improved-facility coverage among other households in the same survey cluster, estimating each under the fixed-effects structure its own construction requires. Household water and sanitation status show no statistically significant association at the 5% level with any of the three outcomes once comparisons are restricted to households within the same cluster (household water–HAZ, *p* = .062, crosses a 10% threshold). Community-level coverage shows a large, significant unadjusted association for most outcome/exposure pairs — the exception is weight-for-height and water, already small and non-significant before adjustment — that attenuates to statistical insignificance once socioeconomic status and region fixed effects are added, a pattern consistent with, though not conclusive evidence of, confounding by local socioeconomic conditions, and one with a specific parallel in panel evidence from 59 countries that similarly finds no sanitation–stunting association once region-level confounding is netted out. A separate, preliminary extension asks, within its own three-country sample, whether modelled local WASH coverage during a child's specific early-life postnatal window is associated with later anthropometric outcomes — a measurement response to one dimension of the single-round baseline design's inability to assign exposure near each child's own developmental period, not an attempt to compare baseline and historical results as a test of exposure timing, since the two samples differ in several other respects (survey rounds, exposure construction, eligibility rules, and control sets) that this design cannot separate from timing. This extension's pooled, established-date water coverage–height-for-age association is positive and statistically significant (β = 1.03 per 10 percentage points of modelled coverage, 95% CI [0.51, 1.54], *p* < .001), independently reconstructed and numerically confirmed with a separate implementation and sample during this project, but it is reported alongside substantial, explicitly documented methodological qualifications: sensitivity to the pooled weighting convention (falling to β = 0.41, 95% CI [0.03, 0.80], *p* = .035, unweighted on the identical observations), an undocumented measurement-subsample sampling weight, and area- rather than population-weighted exposure averaging around confidentiality-displaced survey coordinates. All results in this thesis are associational, estimated under a cross-sectional design, and are not interpreted as causal effects or as evidence either for or against the effectiveness of WASH investment as a policy instrument. The thesis's contribution is a directly comparable household-versus-community WASH analysis within one four-country framework, together with a carefully bounded first attempt at geospatially matched early-life exposure, each reported with its own methodological limitations treated as integral to its interpretation.
