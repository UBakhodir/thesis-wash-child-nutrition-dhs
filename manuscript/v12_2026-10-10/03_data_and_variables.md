# 3. Data and variable construction

This section documents the baseline and historical samples separately throughout. They are built from different DHS rounds, use different exposure constructions, and are validated independently; no claim in this section about one sample should be read as applying to the other. Numerical claims are sourced to the authoritative files listed in Appendix A; this section states what each variable is and how it was constructed, not the estimation results themselves (Section 5).

## 3.1 Baseline sample: unit of analysis, rounds, and pooling

The unit of analysis throughout the baseline sample is the child. The baseline pools the most recent DHS round available, as of data collection, for four countries: Ethiopia (2024–25), Ghana (2022), Kenya (2022), and Nigeria (2024). The full pooled, harmonised child file contains 70,231 children across 4,481 DHS clusters and 114 administrative-region fixed-effect cells (one set of regions per country, pooled). This pooled file is **not** the regression sample for any model: it is the universe before outcome-validity and exposure-classifiability restrictions are applied.

## 3.2 Baseline anthropometric outcomes and under-five eligibility

Three outcomes are constructed following standard DHS/WHO anthropometric practice: height-for-age (HAZ), weight-for-age (WAZ), and weight-for-height (WHZ), each a standard-deviation z-score relative to the WHO Child Growth Standards reference population [@deOnis2006]. A child contributes to an outcome only if that outcome's z-score is flagged valid in the DHS recode (implausible values, per DHS's own flagging convention, are excluded) and the child falls within the DHS-defined under-five anthropometric-measurement universe. Because valid measurements are not available for every child in the pooled file, the outcome-specific baseline samples are smaller than the full pooled file: 36,985 children (HAZ), 37,260 (WAZ), and 37,088 (WHZ). Comparing the household and community specifications on identical observations further requires both exposures to be classifiable for every child in the comparison, giving common baseline samples of 36,040 (HAZ), 36,308 (WAZ), and 36,145 (WHZ), spread across 4,426 DHS clusters. Under-five age is taken uniformly, for all four baseline countries, from the DHS recode's own standard current-age-in-months field (`b19`), the DHS-computed best-available age measure; no country-varying fallback construction is applied in the baseline pipeline. (The historical extension, described separately in Sections 3.5–3.8, constructs its own `age_m` field with a country-varying reported/calendar-difference rule for a different reason specific to that extension's older DHS rounds; the two are distinct constructions over different samples and must not be conflated.)

## 3.3 Baseline WASH exposures: household and leave-one-out community

Four exposures are constructed for the baseline sample: own-household improved/non-improved water, own-household improved/non-improved sanitation, and their leave-one-out community counterparts, each following the WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) "improved" facility classification (piped water, protected wells/springs, and similar for water; sewer/septic and improved latrines for sanitation — the same classification independently verified for the historical extension's gridded product in Section 3.5 and Appendix A). Household WASH is a household-level binary indicator. **Community WASH is an unweighted leave-one-out proportion among other sampled, classifiable HR (Household Recode) households in the same DHS cluster, excluding the child's own household** — not a census of every household physically present in the geographical community, and not an average over the other children in the analytical sample. Concretely: for each child, the numerator and denominator are built from every *other* surveyed household in the cluster whose water or sanitation status is itself classifiable; households recorded as Unknown, structurally not applicable, or — for the sanitation core variant specifically — using an unclassified biodigester, are excluded from **both** the numerator and the denominator of the rate, not merely treated as unimproved. The resulting rate is a proportion on the $[0,1]$ scale; if no other household in the cluster has a classifiable status, the community measure is itself missing for that child, rather than imputed. Improved *shared* sanitation facilities are included in the binary improved category (via a Basic+Limited collapse in the underlying service-level classification, following the JMP convention that shared use of an otherwise-improved facility does not by itself make it unimproved); Ghana's core sanitation classification excludes households using an unclassified biodigester from both numerator and denominator, with a separate robustness variant available that treats the biodigester classification differently. These are the construction choices actually implemented in `scripts/dhs_harmonization/08_hr_cluster_wash_exposure.py`; this section documents them as implemented, not as a recommendation to change them. Two children in the same household never contribute to their own household's community measure, and a child's own classification is never part of its own community exposure. This construction makes community WASH, by design, a property of the cluster that varies mainly *between* clusters rather than within them; Section 4.3 derives precisely how little within-cluster variation the leave-one-out construction leaves and why this rules out a cluster-fixed-effects specification for the community exposure specifically, while it remains appropriate for the household exposure.

## 3.4 Baseline covariates, geography, and weights

Household- and child-level covariates include child sex, the child's own current age in months (`b19`, Section 3.2), maternal age, maternal education (categorical), literacy, household size, birth order, wealth quintile, and urban/rural residence. **The baseline sample is built from country-specific DHS recode files, not from IPUMS-DHS, and uses the DHS recode's own native field names throughout** — these are different fields from the historical extension's IPUMS-harmonized variables described in Section 3.5–3.8, and the two must not be read as interchangeable or as the same underlying field under two names. Specifically: the baseline sample is built from the DHS **Children's Recode (KR)** file — DHS's own distinct Births Recode (BR) is a different file this thesis does not use — whose own weight field is `v005` — **the woman's individual sample weight** (rescaled by the documented factor of 1,000,000 to its usable decimal form, following standard DHS practice), not a separate household-level weight; DHS's household-level weight field, `hv005`, lives only in the Household Recode (HR) file and is not used anywhere in the baseline models. The KR file attaches each birth to its mother's own record, so every child inherits their own mother's `v005` value directly — DHS does not compute or document a separate per-child weight within the KR file specifically, and `v005`, applied this way, is the weight this thesis's child-level baseline analysis actually uses. This is not `PERWEIGHT`, which is a different, IPUMS-harmonized weight field used only by the historical extension. The baseline's cluster identifier is `v021` (DHS's native primary sampling unit code), combined with a country prefix into a country-qualified cluster key (`country_psu`) so that a cluster code never collides across countries, analogous in purpose to the historical extension's `IDHSPSU` but constructed independently from the baseline's own native DHS fields, not derived from or equal to `IDHSPSU` — not the same field under two names. Administrative region is retained as a geographic identifier alongside `country_psu`; DHS cluster GPS coordinates are randomly displaced for confidentiality (urban clusters up to 2 km; rural clusters 99% up to 5 km and 1% up to 10 km; [@Burgert2013]), which is not consequential for the baseline models (which do not use coordinates directly) but is directly consequential for the historical extension's spatial matching (Section 3.5). Baseline models use this `v005`-derived weight, normalised within each model's own estimation sample so that each country contributes an equal total weight mass regardless of its raw sample size — an authorised pooling choice, not a claim that any single country's raw sample size should determine its influence on a pooled coefficient (Section 4.5 gives the exact formula and its consequences).

## 3.5 Historical extension: rounds, exposure source, and scope

The historical extension links an earlier DHS round for three countries — Ghana (2014), Kenya (2014), and Nigeria (2018) — to IHME's annual gridded estimates of local access to improved drinking water (W_IMP) and improved sanitation (S_IMP), available for 2000–2017 [@LBDWaSH2020]. These product definitions were verified directly from the primary source during this project's development, not inferred from the 0–100 raster scale or file names alone: W_IMP is the sum of piped water (on- or off-premises) and other JMP-improved sources (protected wells and springs, bottled water, rainwater collection, bought water); S_IMP is the sum of sewer/septic sanitation and other JMP-improved facilities (improved and ventilated improved latrines, composting toilets), each reported by the source as percent of population with access on a continuous ~5×5 km surface. This project's own spatial extraction computes an **area average of the raster surface within each child's exposure buffer**, not the source study's own population-weighted administrative aggregate; this distinction is maintained throughout and restated wherever a historical coefficient is reported (Section 5.6; Section 6). Ethiopia's 2016 round is included only as a separately labelled, provisional sensitivity analysis (Appendix C), not as part of the established-date pooled sample, because the Ethiopian-calendar-to-Gregorian interview-date conversion required to place Ethiopian children's exposure windows could not be validated to the same standard as the three established-date rounds (Appendix C details the three candidate conversions and why one of them, labelled C1, is invalid and excluded from reporting). As stated in Section 1.2, this construction assigns each child a *modelled, area-level* local coverage estimate for a defined early-life window; it is a measurement improvement on the timing dimension specifically, not a record of what that child's own household facilities actually were, which this or any other available data source cannot provide.

## 3.6 Historical spatial extraction: buffers, displacement, and coverage

Each child's exposure buffer is centred on its DHS cluster's (displaced) coordinates using an azimuthal equidistant (AEQD) projection local to that cluster. **The primary buffer radius is 2 km for urban clusters and 5 km for rural clusters** (`scripts/historical_wash/10_spatial_extraction_v3.py`, `RADIUS_M`); a labelled sensitivity (Section 5.8, Appendix C.4) repeats the rural assignment at 10 km, leaving the urban radius unchanged, and a separate labelled sensitivity uses containing-pixel assignment instead of the buffer average. These settings are stated here explicitly rather than left to be inferred from the DHS displacement distances discussed below, which are a different matter (the *uncertainty* in a cluster's true location) from the buffer radius itself (the *area averaged* around the cluster's recorded location). AEQD preserves true distance and direction from the cluster's own centre point exactly (confirmed by this project's own internal self-test, radial-distance relative error ≈6×10⁻¹²) — it is not, in general, an equal-area projection, and this thesis does not claim it is; what it avoids is the shape/direction (anisotropic) distortion a single, nationally-fixed projection would introduce at different latitudes across four countries (measured directly, up to 15.1% anisotropy at this project's country bounding-box corners for a fixed alternative projection), which at the small buffer radii used here (2–10 km) keeps any residual area distortion negligible as a consequence, not as AEQD's defining property. Within that buffer, the raster's value is averaged using a closed-form disk–polygon overlap computation (not a centroid or nearest-neighbour approximation, and not Monte Carlo sampling): each pixel's true boundary is approximated by the straight-sided quadrilateral formed by projecting its four corner coordinates into the local AEQD plane, and the disk's overlap with that quadrilateral is computed exactly in closed form — "exact" therefore means exact arithmetic applied to this corner-projected approximation of each pixel, not a claim that every pixel's true curved geodetic boundary is captured without approximation. This project's own internal validation (densifying each pixel edge to 16 points as an independent check) found this approximation's own error to be small at the scales used: relative pixel-area error with median ≈1.6×10⁻⁸ and maximum ≈2.1×10⁻⁸, and mean-exposure-value absolute differences with a maximum of 0.001 percentage points. Coverage completeness is checked explicitly: a child-year is assigned a valid extraction only when its valid-pixel area, as a share of the buffer's full nominal area, meets a documented tolerance (within 10⁻³ of full coverage), and partial- or missing-coverage child-years are excluded from that window rather than silently assigned a value computed from incomplete coverage. Separately, the reported mean exposure value for a buffer is itself averaged only over its valid pixels (not diluted by treating missing pixels as zero); this project's own check confirms averaging over the full nominal buffer area instead would shift partial-coverage means by up to 73 percentage points, which is why the valid-area denominator is used for the mean specifically.

## 3.7 Historical exposure windows and completion rules

Four candidate exposure windows are constructed for each child, applied to the same underlying annual raster series: the calendar year of birth; the nine months before birth (prenatal); the first 12 completed postnatal months (the working main window; Section 6.4 states why); and the first 24 postnatal months. Because IHME's product is an annual surface, a window spanning two calendar years is assigned a weighted average of the relevant years' values, weighted by how many of the window's months fall in each calendar year. Every window requires every calendar year it touches to have a verified raster value, but the interview-date completion check itself (excluding a child whose window had not yet elapsed by the time they were interviewed) is **not applied uniformly to all four windows**, and this thesis is explicit about which: it applies to the two postnatal windows (post-12-month and post-24-month) specifically, where it is substantively necessary; the prenatal window requires no separate check because it ends at birth, which a child's interview date — always after their own birth by construction — already guarantees has passed; and the birth-year window, which spans the full calendar year of birth, has no interview-date completion check at all, so a child born partway through a year and interviewed before that year ends is still assigned that year's full annual value. This is a genuine, disclosed limit on what the birth-year window's eligibility rule actually enforces, not an oversight papered over by stating a uniform rule that does not hold.

A related, separate limitation concerns the annual resolution of the exposure surface itself, independent of which completion check applies: because IHME supplies one coverage value per calendar year rather than per month, the annual value used for a window's final touched year can reflect conditions from months that fall *after* a child's own interview date, even when the postnatal completion check above has been satisfied for that window. This project's own construction script flags every such case explicitly (a "post-interview-year-used" indicator, tallied and retained in the data) but does not exclude or adjust any observation on this basis — the flag is descriptive, not a filter. This thesis therefore does not claim that every historical exposure value is strictly measured before the outcome it is matched to; the postnatal completion rule guarantees the *window's defined months* have elapsed by interview, not that the *annual raster value* used to represent the window's final year contains no information from after the interview date. Eligibility for inclusion under any of these rules still requires a valid interview-date conversion — relevant to why Ethiopia's three interview-date candidates matter even for the birth-year window, whose exposure *value* does not itself depend on the precise interview date (Appendix C).

## 3.8 Historical covariates, weights, and the established-date measurement universe

Historical models control for cluster fixed effects, survey-qualified country-by-birth-year terms (each country's own birth-year effects relative to that country's own earliest observed birth year, rather than one reference year shared across all three countries — Section 4.6 describes this convention and reports a dedicated diagnostic establishing that, once the cluster fixed effect and other covariates are accounted for, this specific choice does not itself change the fitted exposure coefficient, though it does change the raw design matrix), child sex, maternal age, maternal education, and marital status, with the child's own completed age in months entered as an additional linear control. **This control is constructed differently from the baseline's age variable, not identically**: the historical extension uses its own `age_m` field — reported completed months where delivered directly by the source recode (Ethiopia, Nigeria), and a documented calendar-month-difference construction otherwise (Ghana, Kenya) — while the baseline uses DHS's own standard `b19` field uniformly for all four countries with no country-varying construction (Section 3.2). The two fields happen to serve an analogous purpose (a linear age-in-months control) but are built by different code over different samples, and neither is a copy of the other's rule. Household wealth quintile is **excluded from the historical extension's main specification**, for a documented, non-fixed-effects reason: current wealth may be a mediator or a post-exposure outcome of the historical WASH exposure being studied, and wealth quintile is correlated with the WASH exposure itself, so including it in the main specification risks conditioning on a variable downstream of the exposure of interest; wealth is included only as a labelled sensitivity check (Section 5.8), where the pooled water–HAZ coefficient is essentially unchanged (1.01 vs. 1.03). This is a different, and more defensible, reason than "cluster fixed effects absorb wealth" — that claim is false in general, since cluster fixed effects absorb only what is constant *within* a cluster, and wealth varies across households within the same cluster just as WASH status does (confirmed directly for this thesis's own baseline cluster-fixed-effects model, where wealth quintile *is* included and estimated as a standard covariate; Section 4.2).

Weighting uses PERWEIGHT, normalised so that each of the three established-date countries contributes an equal total observation weight within each model's own final estimation sample (not population-size weighting) — the same authorised convention as the baseline, carried over for comparability, and reported alongside its unweighted counterpart for the headline cell (Section 5.6). The documented sampling weight for measured anthropometry specifically — as distinct from the general household/individual sampling weight — could not be located in official DHS documentation despite repeated attempts at alternative official sources (Appendix A records the exact sources tried and their outcomes); this project's working inference, stated explicitly wherever historical coefficients are reported, is that within-survey PERWEIGHT normalisation removes a constant measurement-subsample selection factor under an assumption of random household-level selection into the anthropometry subsample, but it cannot and does not correct for any differential non-response among eligible children who were not measured. Historical results are therefore reported as **weighted conditional associations among eligible measured children**, not as population-representative estimates for any defined population, and this phrasing is used consistently rather than varied across sections.

## 3.9 A documented measurement-universe discrepancy: Nigeria 2018

Three distinct child populations arise for Nigeria 2018, and this thesis does not force them to a common count, nor treat any apparent gap between them as resolved. The official 2018 NDHS final report counts 12,806 (unweighted) children under five as eligible for measurement in the survey's anthropometry subsample (households selected for the men's questionnaire, roughly one-third of all households) and actually measured. The IPUMS-DHS birth-history extract used in this thesis contains 33,924 birth-history records for children born within approximately five years of the survey, of which 11,704 are measured or flagged non-missing for anthropometry. The final historical-extension estimation sample (water, HAZ, the main post-12-month window) retains 6,912 children after applying the window-completion, spatial-coverage, and singleton-cluster exclusions described above. These three counts differ by construction — the official count is eligible children in subsample households; the extract is birth-history children of interviewed women, which can omit children whose mothers are not themselves in the sampled frame (a hypothesis raised in this project's own documentation, not independently verified); and the estimation sample further requires complete exposure windows and complete spatial coverage. The gap between the official count and the extract (12,806 vs. 11,704 non-missing) is disclosed here as an open, unresolved measurement-universe question and is not treated as resolved by any estimation choice in this thesis.
