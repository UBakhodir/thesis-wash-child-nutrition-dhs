# 6. Discussion and limitations

## 6.1 Relating the findings to the literature

This thesis's baseline findings sit comfortably within, rather than in tension with, the existing literature. The sharp attenuation of the community WASH–HAZ association from 0.447 (unadjusted) to 0.033 (fully adjusted, not significant) closely parallels Headey and Palloni's (2019) finding, using subnational panel data across 59 countries, that the sanitation–stunting association weakens substantially once richer controls and within-country variation are exploited. The non-significant household cluster-fixed-effects associations (water–HAZ *p* = .062; sanitation–HAZ *p* = .548) are, if anything, more consistent with the major WASH trials' findings (Cumming et al. 2019) — little to no detectable effect on linear growth from WASH interventions as actually delivered and measured — than with the larger unadjusted correlations reported in less-controlled observational work (Günther and Fink 2010; Spears 2013). This thesis does not claim its results confirm the trial literature's finding, since a facility-type classification in cross-sectional survey data and a randomised intervention package are not the same exposure (Section 2.3); but neither does this thesis find evidence that contradicts it. The one statistically significant household-level result in this thesis — household water conditional on community water, HAZ, negative and borderline significant (Section 5.3) — is a single cell among many estimated and is not treated as evidence that improved household water is harmful to child growth; no credible mechanism in the literature reviewed in Section 2 supports such an interpretation, and this thesis does not offer one.

The preliminary historical extension's positive, significant pooled water–HAZ association (1.03 per 10 points, *p* < .001) is the one result across both the baseline and historical analyses that stands out as unlikely to be a multiplicity artefact. It is tempting to read this as evidence that introducing a time dimension — genuine within-cluster, cross-birth-cohort variation — recovers an association the single-round baseline design cannot detect, consistent with this thesis's own motivation in Section 1.2 for attempting the extension at all. This thesis resists that reading for a specific, stated reason (Section 6.2 below): the baseline and historical samples differ along several dimensions simultaneously, not only in whether they have a time dimension, so a difference in results between them cannot be cleanly attributed to exposure timing alone.

## 6.2 Why the baseline and historical results are not directly comparable

The baseline and historical samples use different DHS rounds (2022–2025 vs. 2014–2018), different countries (four vs. three, since Ethiopia is excluded from the historical established-date sample), different exposure measures (own/community household-census-based classification vs. modelled IHME gridded coverage), and different control sets (region or cluster fixed effects with full socioeconomic controls, vs. cluster fixed effects with country-by-birth-year terms and wealth excluded from the main specification). A reader should not conclude from the historical extension's significant pooled result and the baseline's largely non-significant results that exposure timing is "the" explanation for the difference; it is one of at least four things that differ simultaneously between the two analyses, and this thesis's design cannot isolate which of them drives the difference in findings. This is stated plainly here because it is the single most tempting overinterpretation this thesis's own structure invites, and it is not supported by the evidence presented.

## 6.3 Limitations: unresolved documentation

Two issues in this thesis's historical extension remain genuinely unresolved documentation gaps, not design choices or inherent limitations, and could in principle be closed with information this thesis did not succeed in locating. First, no DHS or IPUMS source could be located that documents a measurement-subsample or anthropometry-specific sampling weight, despite repeated attempts at the DHS Biomarker Manual and alternative official sources (Appendix A records the exact sources tried); this thesis's working inference — that within-survey PERWEIGHT normalisation removes a constant measurement-subsample selection factor under an assumption of random household-level selection — is stated as an inference, not a documented fact, and cannot correct for differential non-response among children who were eligible but not measured. Second, the gap between Nigeria 2018's official measured-child count (12,806) and the IPUMS birth-history extract's non-missing anthropometry count (11,704) remains unexplained; a plausible hypothesis (children whose mothers are absent from the birth-history sampling frame) is recorded but not verified. Consistent with both points, this thesis reports its historical coefficients as **weighted conditional associations among eligible measured children**, not as population-representative estimates for any defined population — a phrasing used consistently throughout this chapter and Section 5, and the use of an unweighted estimate alongside the weighted one (Section 5.6) does not resolve this concern, since the selection question is about who was measured at all, not about how measured children are weighted relative to each other.

## 6.4 Limitations: authorised design choices, disclosed and tested

Three features of this thesis's design are deliberate, disclosed choices, not errors, and are discussed here as such rather than apologised for. First, the equal-country-total pooled weighting (Section 4.5) targets an average-country association with each established-date country given equal weight, not a population-size-weighted estimate; its consequence — the pooled water–HAZ coefficient moves from 1.03 (weighted) to 0.41 (unweighted, same observations) — is disclosed and reported alongside the main estimate in every instance, not as a robustness footnote. Second, the working 12-month postnatal exposure window was chosen during the design phase, before any model was estimated, for a documented reason (an early-life window whose completion can be checked against the interview date, avoiding assignment of exposure during an unobserved pregnancy period); it is not described anywhere in this thesis as "preregistered," and the literature basis for this specific window length is modest — Cumming and Cairncross (2016) support early-life exposure generally and, if anything, lean toward a 24-month window rather than 12 specifically — which is why all four candidate windows (Section 5.7) are reported together rather than only the main one. Third, wealth quintile is excluded from the historical extension's main specification because it may be a post-exposure mediator of historical WASH conditions and because it is correlated with the WASH exposure itself (Section 3.8) — a substantive identification concern, not a claim that cluster fixed effects remove wealth mechanically, which this thesis's own baseline model demonstrates is false by including and estimating wealth directly (Section 4.2).

## 6.5 Limitations: inherent, requiring disclosure rather than resolution

A final set of limitations cannot be resolved with the data available to this thesis and are disclosed as permanent features of the design rather than problems awaiting a fix. DHS/IHME input overlap affects two of the three established-date historical countries (Ghana and Kenya), whose own DHS surveys feed into the IHME gridded estimates used as the exposure; an association found in these countries could partly reflect the DHS survey's own contribution to the exposure surface rather than an independent signal, and this thesis reports the overlap-exclusion sensitivity (0.74 for the Ghana-excluded pooled estimate; Section 5.8) alongside the main estimate rather than treating the overlap as removable. Current residence, as recorded at the survey date, is used as a proxy for historical residence during a child's early-life window; any child who migrated between their early-life window and the survey date is assigned the wrong location's exposure, and this cannot be detected or corrected from the available data. DHS cluster coordinates are randomly displaced for confidentiality, so every exposure assigned in this thesis is an area average around a displaced, not a true, location — a standard, accepted feature of all DHS-geolinked analysis, not specific to this project. This thesis's own spatial extraction computes an area average of the raster surface within each buffer, which is a different quantity from the source study's own population-weighted administrative aggregate (Section 3.4); this distinction is maintained rather than collapsed. IHME's exposure surface is itself a *modelled* estimate with its own uncertainty, not a direct observation, and its reported upper/lower confidence bounds are not used as regression inputs in this thesis. The surface is available only at annual resolution, so within-year timing (for example, a child whose postnatal window spans a seasonal change) cannot be captured. The residual within-cluster exposure variation that actually identifies the historical coefficients (0.20–0.88 percentage points depending on country and product) is modest relative to the 10-point scale used for reporting (Section 5.6), a precision limitation rather than a sign of non-identification, but one that should temper how large an effect the pooled coefficient is read as describing. Finally, Ethiopia's interview-date uncertainty (Section 5.9; Appendix C) is treated as an inherent limitation of that round's recoded date fields, not a design choice this thesis made, and is the reason Ethiopia remains excluded from the established-date pooled sample throughout.

## 6.6 What this thesis does not claim

Given the above, several claims are explicitly not made anywhere in this thesis and are flagged here to prevent misreading. This thesis does not claim that its findings establish a causal effect of WASH facilities on child growth, in either direction. It does not claim that its null or weak baseline associations are evidence that WASH investment is an ineffective use of development resources — a materially different, and much stronger, claim that this four-country, cross-sectional, facility-classification design cannot support, and one the broader literature (Section 2.3) does not support uniformly either. It does not generalise findings from Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya, and Nigeria to Sub-Saharan Africa as a region. It does not treat the historical extension's computational verification (Appendix B) as evidence of improved causal identification; computational verification establishes that a reported number is what the stated specification, correctly implemented, actually produces, nothing more.
