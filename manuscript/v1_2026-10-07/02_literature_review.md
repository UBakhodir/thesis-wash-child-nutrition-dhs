# 2. Literature review and conceptual framework

## 2.1 From biological plausibility to demonstrated association

The hypothesised pathway from WASH to child growth is well established biologically: inadequate water and sanitation increase exposure to faecal–oral pathogens, raising the frequency of diarrhoeal disease and sub-clinical environmental enteric dysfunction, which together redirect energy and nutrients away from linear growth (Cumming and Cairncross 2016). Black et al. (2013) place WASH-related infection among the proximate determinants of child undernutrition in their widely cited *Lancet* framework, alongside inadequate diet and maternal and child care practices, and Victora et al. (2010) document that growth faltering is concentrated in the window from birth to roughly 24 months, which is also the period in which cumulative infectious exposure is thought to matter most for linear growth. This biological plausibility is not in serious dispute. What is considerably less settled is whether it translates into a *detectable, policy-relevant statistical association* in observational data once the obvious confounders — household wealth, maternal education, and local infrastructure and health-service access, all of which are correlated with both WASH status and child nutrition for reasons unrelated to any WASH-specific pathway — are properly accounted for. This thesis is concerned with that second, narrower, empirical question, not with re-litigating the first.

## 2.2 Observational evidence and the confounding problem

A substantial observational literature finds WASH variables associated with child anthropometric outcomes in DHS and related survey data. Rakotomanana et al. (2020) link WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme water and sanitation indicators to linear growth in children aged 6–23 months across several East African DHS surveys and find associations in the expected direction. Addae et al. (2024), using Ghana's most recent national survey, find household water, sanitation, and socioeconomic status jointly associated with stunting and wasting among children aged 6–23 months. At a larger scale, Günther and Fink (2010) pool 172 DHS surveys and estimate that improved water and sanitation are associated with meaningfully lower child mortality, and Spears (2013) documents that cross-country variation in average child height correlates strongly with cross-country variation in open-defecation rates — a correlation large enough, Spears argues, to be difficult to explain by wealth differences alone, though the analysis remains observational and cross-sectional at the country level.

The central methodological difficulty common to this literature, and squarely faced in this thesis, is that household and community WASH status are not randomly assigned: both are strongly correlated with household wealth, parental education, and the general level of local infrastructure and public-service provision, all of which independently predict child nutrition through channels that have nothing to do with pathogen exposure. Headey and Palloni (2019) address this directly using subnational panel data across 59 countries and find that the cross-sectional association between sanitation coverage and stunting weakens substantially, though does not vanish, once they exploit within-country, over-time variation and control for a richer set of covariates — a pattern this thesis's own baseline results echo closely (Section 5): the community-WASH association here falls from a large, highly significant 0.447 (water, HAZ) in a minimally adjusted model to a statistically insignificant 0.033 once socioeconomic status and region fixed effects are added. This attenuation pattern recurs across the observational WASH literature closely enough that it should be treated as a stylised fact the literature has already established, not a surprising finding specific to this thesis's four countries.

## 2.3 Credible causal evidence: what the major trials actually found

Observational association is one evidentiary standard; randomised evidence is another, and the two have told a more complicated story than the biological mechanism alone would suggest. Cumming et al. (2019), summarising three major cluster-randomised WASH trials (WASH Benefits in Kenya and Bangladesh, and SHINE in Zimbabwe), report that water, sanitation, and hygiene interventions as delivered in these trials had **little to no detectable effect on child linear growth**, even though some components reduced diarrhoeal disease incidence in at least one setting. This is an important and sometimes under-emphasised finding for a thesis built on observational WASH–growth associations: the strongest available causal evidence does not straightforwardly confirm that improving household-level WASH facilities, as typically implemented and measured, produces detectable gains in child height over the trial periods studied. Cumming and Cairncross (2016), writing before these trial results were available, had already cautioned that achieving the hypothesised stunting-reduction effect of WASH interventions likely requires addressing exposure pathways more comprehensively than a single facility upgrade — community-wide sanitation coverage, safe water at the point of use, and hygiene behaviour together — a caution the subsequent trial evidence is broadly consistent with.

A related quasi-experimental literature targets a different outcome — infant mortality rather than anthropometric growth — but is worth noting for its identification strategy. Geruso and Spears (2015, revised 2017; this thesis cites the working-paper version specifically, since only that version was read and verified during source preparation, and page-specific claims from it should not be assumed to match the later published article's pagination) exploit neighbourhood-level sanitation differences in India, using a design intended to isolate sanitation's own contribution from general neighbourhood development, and find a sanitation-specific association with infant mortality. This is cited here as an example of the kind of identification strategy — exploiting fine-grained local variation net of broader development controls — that the WASH–nutrition literature more generally still lacks for anthropometric outcomes specifically, not as direct evidence about child growth.

This matters directly for how this thesis's own results should be read. The exposure measured throughout this thesis — whether a household has an "improved" water source or sanitation facility, in the standard WHO/UNICEF JMP classification sense — is a facility-type classification, not a measure of a comprehensive WASH *intervention package* of the kind tested in the major trials. A null or weak association between facility classification and child growth in this thesis's data is therefore consistent with, but does not on its own establish, the trial literature's finding that facility-level WASH improvements alone are often insufficient; it is equally consistent with confounding in the observational data masking a real effect, with measurement error in the classification, or with this thesis's own specific limitations (Section 6). This thesis does not interpret its null or weak findings as evidence that WASH investment is ineffective, and the distinction between "intervention package" and "improved-facility classification" is maintained throughout rather than collapsed, as it sometimes is in secondary discussions of this literature.

## 2.4 Household versus community exposure

Most of the DHS-based observational literature measures WASH at the household level only. A smaller, more recent strand examines community-level or spatially aggregated WASH exposure as a distinct construct. Donohue, Church, Assaf, and Mayala (2023), in a DHS Spatial Analysis Report, construct community-level improved-sanitation coverage around DHS clusters and relate it to childhood stunting, finding that community coverage carries explanatory relevance beyond a child's own household facilities — directly motivating this thesis's decision to construct and report both exposures rather than only the household measure. Skoufias and Vinha (2026) develop a related community-externality framework for child health in Sub-Saharan Africa, formalising the intuition that a child's own household facilities may not fully protect against environmental contamination when neighbouring households lack improved facilities. This thesis's leave-one-out community construction — community coverage calculated from every *other* household in a child's DHS cluster, excluding the child's own household, so that no child's own classification enters its own community exposure — follows this same logic and is, to the author's knowledge, not a construction that has been directly paired with a household-level comparison in the same four-country sample before. Balk et al. (2004), working on child mortality in West Africa with spatially referenced survey data, is an earlier methodological precedent for treating DHS-cluster-level geography as a unit of analysis distinct from the household, though applied to mortality rather than anthropometric outcomes.

A specific identification issue arises when community WASH is measured this way: because the leave-one-out rate is itself a property of the cluster rather than of the individual child, attempting to estimate it with cluster fixed effects — the natural approach for household WASH — would remove nearly all of its substantive variation, leaving a coefficient identified almost entirely by the small mechanical residual that arises because a different household is excluded from each child's own calculation (Section 4 derives this precisely, with the within- versus between-cluster standard deviations that demonstrate it). This thesis therefore uses region, not cluster, fixed effects for the community specification — a design choice forced by the construction of the exposure itself, not a weaker alternative chosen for convenience.

## 2.5 Early-life timing and the limits of a single survey round

Every study reviewed above shares a measurement limitation with this thesis's own baseline analysis: WASH status is recorded once, typically at the survey date, and related to the current anthropometric status of children of varying current ages, some of whom experienced their nutritionally critical early-life period years before the survey. Age controls and age-interaction terms — which this thesis also estimates (Section 4, Section 5) — describe whether the *current* survey-date association differs by how old a child is today; they do not reconstruct what a child's own environment was during their own early life, and this thesis is explicit that these are different empirical questions (Section 1.2). This is a limitation of the cross-sectional DHS design generally, not one specific to any study's choices, and it is the reason this thesis attempts a historical, time-varying exposure extension at all.

## 2.6 Geospatial exposure assignment, and a matching-method precedent from outside the WASH literature

Assigning a continuous, gridded exposure surface to a survey respondent's location requires handling two well-documented DHS features: coordinates are randomly displaced for confidentiality (Burgert, Colston, Roy, and Zachary 2013; Perez-Heydrich, Warren, Burgert, and Emch 2013), so any exposure assigned to a cluster's recorded coordinates is necessarily an area average around a displaced, not a true, location; and gridded surfaces must be aggregated to that area in a way that is explicit about whether the aggregation is a simple area average or a population-weighted one, a distinction this thesis's own data chapter makes explicit for its own extraction method (Section 3.4). Johnson, Jacob, and Brown (2013) provide an earlier precedent for linking satellite-derived environmental surfaces (forest cover) to DHS cluster locations to study a different child-health outcome, establishing that this general linkage approach has methodological grounding independent of the specific exposure being matched. Blom, Ortiz-Bobea, and Hoddinott (2022) match historical, geocoded weather data to each child's own developmental window across repeated DHS rounds — a design this thesis's historical extension borrows structurally (matching a time-varying, geocoded exposure to each child's own early-life window rather than to the survey date). This citation is used here, and only here, for its **matching methodology**: temperature is continuously observed over the full historical period in a way that WASH coverage, estimated only from 2000 onward by the data source used in this thesis, is not, and nothing in Blom, Ortiz-Bobea, and Hoddinott (2022) is evidence about WASH or nutrition specifically.

## 2.7 This thesis's contribution and the gap it addresses

Positioned against this literature, this thesis's contribution is narrow and should be described as such. It is not the first study to measure household WASH and child anthropometry in DHS data (Rakotomanana et al. 2020; Addae et al. 2024), nor the first to examine community-level WASH coverage (Donohue et al. 2023; Skoufias and Vinha 2026), nor the first to attempt geospatial exposure matching to DHS clusters (Johnson, Jacob, and Brown 2013; Balk et al. 2004) or to a child's own developmental window using a time-varying surface (Blom, Ortiz-Bobea, and Hoddinott 2022, for a different exposure). What this thesis does is combine these three elements — household and leave-one-out community WASH compared directly within the same sample and controls, and a preliminary, transparently bounded attempt to extend the comparison with time-varying, geospatially matched historical WASH coverage for three of the four countries — within one four-country DHS analysis, while being explicit throughout about which parts of the design are validated and ready for substantive reporting and which remain exploratory (Section 5; Appendix C). The gap this leaves open, and does not claim to close, is the same one Headey and Palloni (2019) and the WASH-trial literature (Cumming et al. 2019) already identify at larger scale: credibly separating a WASH-specific effect from the broader package of local development that improved facilities tend to accompany. Nothing in this thesis's four-country, cross-sectional design resolves that identification problem; the historical extension narrows the exposure-timing dimension of it without resolving the confounding dimension, and this thesis does not represent otherwise.
