# 1. Introduction

Child stunting remains one of the most persistent markers of population health disadvantage in Sub-Saharan Africa. Linear growth faltering concentrates in the first two years of life and is difficult to reverse once established, with consequences that extend well beyond anthropometry into cognitive development, school attainment, and adult earning capacity (Victora et al. 2008; Victora et al. 2010; Black et al. 2013). Among the environmental exposures proposed to explain why children in similar economic circumstances grow at such different rates, water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) conditions occupy a central place in both the public-health and development-economics literatures. The proposed mechanism is biologically direct: inadequate water and sanitation increase a child's exposure to faecal pathogens, raising the frequency of diarrhoeal and sub-clinical enteric infection, which in turn diverts nutritional intake toward immune response rather than growth — a pathway often described as environmental enteric dysfunction (Cumming and Cairncross 2016). Whether this plausible biological pathway translates into a detectable, policy-relevant association in observational survey data, once the obvious confounding by household wealth and local development is accounted for, is considerably less settled, and is the question this thesis investigates.

## 1.1 Two distinct exposures: a household's own facilities and its surroundings

A household's own water source and sanitation facility are not the only WASH-related exposure a child faces. Even a child in a household with an improved latrine and piped water can be exposed to pathogens through shared water sources, open defecation by neighbours, flooding, or simply proximity to inadequately managed waste if the surrounding community lacks similar facilities. This distinction — between **own-household WASH** as a private good and **surrounding-community WASH** as a local environmental condition — is not a modelling convenience; it corresponds to two different exposure pathways with different plausible causal structures, and the literature on community-level WASH externalities (Donohue et al. 2023; Skoufias and Vinha 2026) suggests the two need not move together or carry the same association with child growth. This thesis constructs and compares both: a child's own household classification, and a leave-one-out measure of improved-facility coverage among every *other* household in the same DHS cluster, so that a child's own household is never used to construct its own community exposure.

## 1.2 The exposure-timing problem

A second limitation runs through almost all cross-sectional DHS-based WASH studies, including the baseline analysis in this thesis: household and community WASH status is recorded once, at the survey date, and applied uniformly to every child in that round regardless of the child's age. A four-year-old measured in a 2022 survey experienced their nutritionally critical first two years of life around 2018–2019, under WASH conditions the survey-date measurement cannot describe, while an infant measured in the same round has WASH recorded very close to their own early life. Age controls and age-interaction tests describe how the *current* survey-date association varies with how old a child is today; they do not reconstruct what a particular child's own environment actually was during their own developmental window. This thesis addresses this limitation in two stages. The main, baseline analysis documents the limitation explicitly and does not claim to resolve it. A separate, clearly labelled historical extension matches each child's birth-cohort location to multi-year, modelled local WASH coverage estimates (IHME's gridded 2000–2017 drinking-water and sanitation access surfaces; Local Burden of Disease WaSH Collaborators 2020) for a defined early-life window, introducing genuine within-cluster, cross-birth-cohort temporal variation that the single-round design cannot offer. This extension is described precisely as what it is — a modelled, area-level estimate of local WASH coverage during a child's early life, not a reconstruction of any individual household's actual historical facilities, which no available data source can provide.

## 1.3 Research questions

This thesis asks three related questions. First, is a child's own household's water and sanitation status associated with their anthropometric outcomes (height-for-age, weight-for-age, weight-for-height), after accounting for household and cluster-level confounding? Second, is the WASH status of the surrounding community — independent of the child's own household — separately associated with these outcomes? Third, in a preliminary and clearly bounded extension, does matching children to modelled local WASH coverage during a specific early-life window, rather than to a single survey-date measurement, change what these associations look like? All three questions are posed and answered as **associations under a cross-sectional design**, not as estimates of causal effects; this is stated once here and governs how every coefficient in this thesis should be read.

## 1.4 Scope: four countries, two time horizons

The baseline analysis pools the most recent Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) round available, as of the data-collection date, for four countries: Ethiopia (2024–25), Ghana (2022), Kenya (2022), and Nigeria (2024). These four countries were selected for data availability and recency, not as a representative sample of Sub-Saharan Africa, and no claim in this thesis generalises findings from these four countries to the region as a whole. The historical extension uses an earlier DHS round for three of the four countries — Ghana (2014), Kenya (2014), and Nigeria (2018) — linked to IHME's annual gridded WASH-coverage estimates, which are available through 2017. Ethiopia's 2016 round is retained only as a separately labelled, provisional sensitivity analysis, because the Ethiopian-calendar interview-date conversion required to place Ethiopian children in a specific early-life calendar window could not be fully validated (Section 3.5 and Appendix C). The baseline and historical samples are **not the same children** and are analysed, validated, and reported separately throughout; no result in one sample is used to adjust, select, or validate a result in the other.

## 1.5 Contribution

Relative to the observational WASH–growth literature this thesis builds on — large multi-country panel work (Headey and Palloni 2019; Günther and Fink 2010), cross-sectional DHS analyses (Rakotomanana et al. 2020; Addae et al. 2024), and community-level spatial WASH studies (Donohue et al. 2023; Skoufias and Vinha 2026) — this thesis's specific contribution is the explicit, side-by-side comparison of own-household and leave-one-out community WASH exposure within the same four-country sample and the same set of controls, together with a transparently documented, preliminary attempt to introduce genuine temporal exposure variation through geospatially matched historical coverage data. This is a modest, clearly bounded contribution, not a claim to resolve the exposure-timing problem that affects this entire literature; the historical extension is reported as exploratory throughout, and its methodological limitations (Section 6) are treated as integral to its interpretation, not as footnotes to be read around.

## 1.6 A restrained summary of findings

Stated briefly here and developed with full uncertainty in Sections 5–6: household-level WASH shows no statistically significant association with height-for-age once cluster fixed effects are used to compare households within the same community (water: β = −0.085, 95% CI [−0.175, 0.004], *p* = .062; sanitation: β = 0.026, 95% CI [−0.058, 0.110], *p* = .548). Community-level WASH coverage shows a large, strongly significant unadjusted association that attenuates to statistical insignificance once socioeconomic controls and region fixed effects are added (water: 0.447 unadjusted vs. 0.033 adjusted, *p* = .573; sanitation: 0.472 unadjusted vs. 0.008 adjusted, *p* = .894) — a pattern consistent with, but not conclusive proof of, confounding by local socioeconomic conditions. The preliminary historical extension's pooled established-date water–HAZ association is positive and significant (β per 10 percentage points of modelled coverage = 1.03, 95% CI [0.51, 1.54], *p* < .001) but is sensitive to the weighting convention used (falling to 0.41 unweighted on the same observations) and carries a substantial list of unresolved documentation issues (Section 6; Appendix C). None of these results should be read as evidence either for or against the effectiveness of WASH investment as a policy instrument; they describe statistical associations in four specific survey samples, nothing more.

## 1.7 Design and principal limitations, stated at the outset

This is a cross-sectional, associational design throughout. It is not a panel, not a difference-in-differences design, and not an instrumental-variables design, and none of those terms is used elsewhere in this thesis to describe a method that is not actually implemented. The principal limitations — unresolved sampling-weight documentation for the historical extension's measured-anthropometry subsample, DHS/IHME input overlap in two of the three historical-extension countries, the use of current residence as a proxy for historical residence, confidentiality-driven displacement of DHS cluster coordinates, and the small number of countries in both samples — are stated in full in Section 6 and are not treated there as a closing formality.

## 1.8 A note on the thesis title

This thesis was registered under the title *"The Impact of Improved Water and Sanitation on Child Nutritional Outcomes in Sub-Saharan Africa: Evidence from DHS and Geospatial Data."* That title is retained unchanged throughout this manuscript, as no authorisation to change it has been obtained. Given that every result reported here is an association under a cross-sectional design rather than an estimate of a causal impact, and that the four-country sample is not representative of Sub-Saharan Africa as a region, the author suggests — for the supervisor's review, not as an adopted change — that a title such as *"Household and Community Water and Sanitation Conditions and Child Nutritional Outcomes: Evidence from Four Sub-Saharan African Countries"* would more precisely describe the thesis's actual design and scope. This suggestion is recorded here for discussion and is not acted upon elsewhere in the manuscript.

## 1.9 Structure

Section 2 situates this thesis within the WASH–child-growth literature, distinguishing biological plausibility from demonstrated empirical association, and causal evidence from conditional association. Section 3 describes the data, sample construction, and variable definitions for both the baseline and historical samples. Section 4 sets out the empirical strategy, with notation matched to the actual estimating equations. Section 5 reports results. Section 6 discusses findings in relation to the literature and states limitations in full. Section 7 concludes.
