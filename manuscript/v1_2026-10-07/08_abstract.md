# Abstract

*(Written after all other chapters, to match the balanced evidence actually presented in Sections 5–7, rather than anticipating it.)*

This thesis examines associations between household and community water and sanitation conditions and child anthropometric outcomes (height-for-age, weight-for-age, weight-for-height) using Demographic and Health Survey data from four Sub-Saharan African countries: Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya, and Nigeria. It distinguishes throughout between a child's own household facilities and a leave-one-out measure of improved-facility coverage among other households in the same survey cluster, and estimates both under region and cluster fixed effects as appropriate to each exposure's construction. Household water and sanitation status show no statistically significant association with any of the three outcomes once comparisons are restricted to households within the same cluster. Community-level coverage shows a large, significant unadjusted association that attenuates to statistical insignificance once socioeconomic status and region fixed effects are added, a pattern consistent with, though not conclusive evidence of, confounding by local socioeconomic conditions. A preliminary extension links three of the four countries' earlier survey rounds to annual, geospatially modelled local WASH coverage estimates for a defined early-life window, in an attempt to address the single-round design's inability to measure WASH exposure during a child's own developmental period rather than at the survey date. This extension's pooled, established-date water coverage–height-for-age association is positive and statistically significant (β = 1.03 per 10 percentage points of modelled coverage, 95% CI [0.51, 1.54], *p* < .001), independently reconstructed and numerically confirmed with a separate implementation and sample during this project, but it is reported alongside substantial, explicitly documented methodological qualifications: sensitivity to the pooled weighting convention, an undocumented measurement-subsample sampling weight, area- rather than population-weighted exposure averaging around confidentiality-displaced survey coordinates, and an exposure-timing design that cannot be cleanly isolated from the several other ways the historical and baseline samples differ. All results in this thesis are associational, estimated under a cross-sectional design, and are not interpreted as causal effects or as evidence either for or against the effectiveness of WASH investment as a policy instrument. The thesis's contribution is a transparent, directly comparable household-versus-community WASH analysis within one four-country framework, together with a carefully bounded first attempt at geospatially matched historical exposure, reported with the methodological limitations of both treated as integral to their interpretation rather than as closing caveats.
