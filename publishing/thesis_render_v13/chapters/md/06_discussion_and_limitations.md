# 6. Discussion and limitations

## 6.1 Relating the findings to the literature

The baseline findings are consistent with the existing literature, but only narrowly. Headey and Palloni [-@HeadeyPalloni2019], under a within-region panel design netting out time-invariant confounding, find sanitation improvements predict large reductions in diarrhoea and mortality but **not** stunting or wasting, while water is insignificant for most outcomes except piped water. Their null sanitation–stunting finding parallels this thesis's own null household- and community-sanitation associations with HAZ (Sections 5.1–5.2), each via its own design (region-level panel fixed effects there; region or cluster fixed effects here) — narrower than claiming WASH–growth associations generally attenuate under better controls, which would overstate what one study establishes.

The household cluster-fixed-effects associations (Table 5.1) are not significant at 5%, broadly consistent with the strongest randomised evidence available: no WASH-only arm in the three trials showed a detectable growth effect (Section 2.3), and adding WASH to nutrition brought no additional benefit over nutrition alone [@Cumming2019]. This is not confirmation — a facility-type classification in cross-sectional data and a randomised prospective intervention answer related but distinct questions. The one significant household-level result (Section 5.3) is negative and just below threshold, one cell among twelve, not evidence improved household water harms growth; no mechanism in Section 2 supports that reading.

The historical extension's pooled water–HAZ association is positive and significant (Section 5.6), independently reproduced with a second estimation library on a separately rebuilt sample, as were all 24 primary cells (Appendix B). Computational reproduction is not a multiplicity correction: no formal adjustment has been applied across the baseline and historical analyses, so this thesis has neither quantified nor ruled out that this result is one significant draw among many tested cells. It would be tempting to read it as evidence that a time dimension recovers an association the single-round baseline cannot detect; Section 6.2 explains why this thesis does not draw that conclusion.

## 6.2 Why baseline and historical results cannot test exposure timing

This is the most tempting overinterpretation the thesis's structure invites. The historical extension's research question (Section 1.3) is framed to avoid it: it asks whether modelled early-life WASH coverage is associated with outcomes *within its own sample*, not whether assigning a historical rather than current exposure changes an otherwise identical estimate.

The baseline and historical samples differ in at least four respects at once:

1. **Children and survey rounds.** The baseline uses 2022–2025 rounds from four countries; the historical extension uses 2014–2018 rounds from three, Ethiopia entering only as a provisional sensitivity (Section 1.3). No child appears in both.
2. **Exposure definitions.** The baseline exposure is each household's own survey response, or an unweighted leave-one-out proportion among other households (Section 3.3); the historical exposure is a modelled, area-averaged annual gridded estimate matched to a postnatal window (Sections 3.5–3.7) — a different kind of measurement, not the same quantity at a different time.
3. **Eligibility rules.** The historical sample requires complete spatial coverage and a complete window verified against the interview date (Section 3.7), with no baseline counterpart.
4. **Control sets.** The baseline household model uses cluster fixed effects with socioeconomic controls (Section 3.4); region fixed effects appear in the region and community specifications but are never stacked with cluster fixed effects. The historical specification uses cluster fixed effects with country-by-birth-year terms, excludes wealth for its own documented reason (Sections 3.8, 4.6), and is identified by genuine within-cluster cohort variation; the baseline community model is deliberately not estimated with cluster fixed effects (Section 4.3), for a reason with no bearing on the historical design.

Because all four differ at once, the contrast between the baseline's largely null associations and the historical headline cannot be attributed to "the effect of assigning historical instead of current exposure" — that headline is one of six nominally significant cells among 24, not the only significant result (Section 5.6). The contrast could instead, or in addition, reflect different confounding structures, different measurement-error properties, the different control sets, or chance given the number of cells estimated. No formal test of the baseline-versus-historical difference was carried out. This thesis does not adjudicate between these explanations: its design, two separately constructed samples rather than one in which only timing varies, cannot do so.

**The within-historical-sample window comparison (Section 5.7)** holds constant the survey rounds, exposure source, control set, and estimator, changing only which months are averaged — but not the estimation sample, since each window has its own eligible children. With the window held at post-12-month, the estimate moves from 1.03 on its own eligible sample to 1.64 on the smaller common-eligible sample, a sample-composition effect, not evidence favouring one window. This comparison says nothing about the baseline-versus-historical contrast above.

## 6.3 Limitations: unresolved documentation

Two issues remain genuinely unresolved, neither a design choice nor an inherent limitation. First, no DHS or IPUMS source documents a sampling weight specific to the measured-anthropometry subsample (Appendix A); the working inference, not a documented fact, is that within-survey PERWEIGHT normalisation removes a constant selection factor under random household-level selection, though it cannot correct for differential non-response among eligible but unmeasured children. Second, the gap between Nigeria 2018's official measured-child count (12,806) and the IPUMS extract's non-missing-anthropometry count (11,704) remains unexplained (Section 3.9). Both bear on which children the historical estimates describe, so results are reported as **weighted conditional associations among eligible measured children**, not population-representative estimates; if measurement or non-response related to both WASH coverage and growth, the estimated associations could differ from the full eligible population in a direction the data cannot show.

## 6.4 Limitations: authorised design choices

Three features are deliberate, disclosed choices, not errors. First, equal-country-total weighting (Section 4.5) is not a population-size-weighted estimate or an average of separately estimated country coefficients — statistical influence also depends on residual exposure variation, so equal weight totals do not imply equal influence; the pooled water–HAZ coefficient moves from 1.03 (weighted) to 0.41 (unweighted), neither population-representative. Second, the working 12-month postnatal window was proposed as primary in a dated specification document before any model was estimated, because its completion can be checked against the interview date; that document explicitly left the final choice open for author/supervisor input (not resolved until later), so this thesis describes the window as proposed, not as "preregistered" or finally decided in advance. The literature basis is modest — Cumming and Cairncross [-@CummingCairncross2016] lean toward a 24-month window — which is why all four candidate windows (Section 5.7) are reported together. Third, wealth quintile is excluded from the historical main specification because it may be a post-exposure mediator correlated with the exposure (Section 3.8), not a claim that cluster fixed effects remove wealth mechanically (Section 4.2 shows otherwise); the competing concern is that current wealth could instead proxy prior socioeconomic circumstances, left unaddressed if excluded. Adding wealth leaves the coefficient essentially unchanged (1.01 vs. 1.03; Section 5.8), without adjudicating between the two readings.

## 6.5 Limitations: inherent, not resolvable

DHS/IHME input overlap affects Ghana and Kenya, whose own DHS surveys feed the IHME estimates used as exposure; the overlap-exclusion sensitivity (pooled water–HAZ excluding Ghana: 0.74, Section 5.8) addresses only part of it, since Kenya's overlap remains. Current residence proxies early-life residence; children whose survey residence differs from their early-life residence may be assigned an exposure that misrepresents it, under both the buffer-average and containing-pixel methods — a measurement error of unknown extent. DHS cluster coordinates are randomly displaced for confidentiality; neither extraction method recovers the true location. The IHME surface is itself a modelled estimate with its own uncertainty not reflected in the reported standard errors, and its annual resolution means a window's final year can include months after interview even when the postnatal completion rule is satisfied (flagged, not excluded or adjusted for). The residual within-cluster exposure variation that identifies the historical coefficients is small (0.20–0.88 percentage points by country and product; 0.43 for the pooled water–HAZ model) relative to the 10-point reporting convention. Finally, Ethiopia's interview-date uncertainty is inherent to that round's recoded date fields, not a design choice; its estimates remain provisional and outside the established-date pooled family.

## 6.6 What this thesis does not claim

This thesis does not claim its findings establish a causal effect of WASH facilities on child growth, in either direction: both analyses are observational, and fixed effects remove only what is shared within a region or cluster, leaving within-cluster confounding possible (Section 4.2). Nor does it read the null or weak baseline associations as evidence WASH investment is ineffective, generalise findings from these four countries to Sub-Saharan Africa (Section 1.3), treat computational verification (Appendix B) as evidence of improved causal identification, or treat the baseline/historical contrast as evidence that exposure timing explains it (Section 6.2).
