# 3. Data and variable construction

This section documents the baseline and historical samples separately throughout. The two samples are built from different DHS rounds, use different exposure constructions, and are validated independently; no claim in this section about one sample should be read as applying to the other. Numerical claims are sourced to the authoritative files listed in Appendix A. This section states what each variable is and how it was constructed; the estimation results themselves are reported in Section 5.

## 3.1 Baseline sample: unit of analysis, rounds, and pooling

The unit of analysis throughout the baseline sample is the child. The baseline pools the most recent DHS round available, as of data collection, for four countries: Ethiopia (2024–25), Ghana (2022), Kenya (2022), and Nigeria (2024). The full pooled, harmonised child file contains 70,231 children across 4,481 DHS clusters and 114 administrative-region fixed-effect cells (one set of regions per country, pooled). This pooled file is **not** the regression sample for any model. It is the universe before outcome-validity and exposure-classifiability restrictions are applied.

## 3.2 Baseline anthropometric outcomes and under-five eligibility

Three outcomes are constructed according to standard DHS/WHO anthropometric practice: height-for-age (HAZ), weight-for-age (WAZ), and weight-for-height (WHZ). Each outcome is a standard-deviation z-score relative to the WHO Child Growth Standards reference population [@deOnis2006]. A child contributes to an outcome only if that outcome's z-score is flagged valid in the DHS recode: the implementing code applies one rule, excluding DHS's own documented special codes (9996–9999) and system-missing values, with no separate measurement-universe filter applied in code. Restriction to the under-five anthropometric-measurement universe is, in practice, a property of which children DHS itself records these fields for, not an additional filtering step this pipeline applies. Because valid measurements are not available for every child in the pooled file, the outcome-specific baseline samples are smaller than the full pooled file: 36,985 children (HAZ), 37,260 (WAZ), and 37,088 (WHZ). Comparing the household and community specifications on identical observations further requires both exposures to be classifiable for every child in the comparison. This gives common baseline samples of 36,040 (HAZ), 36,308 (WAZ), and 36,145 (WHZ), spread across 4,426 DHS clusters. Under-five age is taken uniformly, for all four baseline countries, from the DHS recode's own standard current-age-in-months field (`b19`), the DHS-computed best-available age measure; the baseline pipeline applies no country-varying fallback construction. (The historical extension, described separately in Sections 3.5–3.8, constructs its own `age_m` field with a country-varying reported/calendar-difference rule, for a reason specific to that extension's older DHS rounds. The two are distinct constructions over different samples and must not be conflated.)

## 3.3 Baseline WASH exposures: household and leave-one-out community

The baseline sample has four exposures: own-household improved/non-improved water and sanitation, and their leave-one-out community counterparts, each following the WHO/UNICEF JMP "improved" facility classification (piped water, protected wells/springs, etc. for water; sewer/septic and improved latrines for sanitation), the same classification independently verified for the historical extension's gridded product (Section 3.5, Appendix A). Household WASH is a household-level binary indicator. **Community WASH is an unweighted leave-one-out proportion among other sampled, classifiable HR households in the same DHS cluster, excluding the child's own household** — not a census of the geographical community, and not an average over other children in the analytical sample. For each child, the numerator and denominator are built from every *other* surveyed household in the cluster whose status is itself classifiable; households recorded as Unknown or not applicable, and (for Ghana's sanitation core variant) an unclassified biodigester, are excluded from **both** numerator and denominator, not merely treated as unimproved. The resulting rate is a $[0,1]$ proportion, missing (not imputed) if no other classifiable household exists in the cluster. Improved *shared* sanitation is included in the improved category via a Basic+Limited collapse, following the JMP convention that shared use alone does not make an otherwise-improved facility unimproved; a separate robustness variant treats Ghana's biodigester classification differently. These are the construction choices implemented in `scripts/dhs_harmonization/08_hr_cluster_wash_exposure.py`, documented here as implemented. A child's own classification is never part of its own community exposure, which by design makes community WASH vary mainly *between* clusters rather than within them — Section 4.3 derives how little within-cluster variation this leaves, and why it rules out a cluster-fixed-effects specification for this exposure specifically, unlike for household WASH.

## 3.4 Baseline covariates, geography, and weights

Household- and child-level covariates include child sex, current age in months (`b19`), maternal age, maternal education (categorical), literacy, household size, birth order, wealth quintile, and urban/rural residence. **The baseline sample is built from country-specific DHS recode files, not IPUMS-DHS**, using the DHS recode's own native field names throughout — different from, and not interchangeable with, the historical extension's IPUMS-harmonized variables (Sections 3.5–3.8). Specifically, it is built from the DHS **Children's Recode (KR)** file (DHS's distinct Births Recode, BR, is not used). The KR file's own weight field is `v005`, **the woman's individual sample weight** (rescaled by the documented 1,000,000 factor), not a household-level weight (`hv005`, which exists only in the Household Recode and is unused here); the KR file attaches each birth to its mother's record, so every child inherits her `v005` directly, and DHS documents no separate per-child weight. This is not `PERWEIGHT`, the different, IPUMS-harmonized field the historical extension uses. The cluster identifier is `v021` (DHS's native PSU code), combined with a country prefix into `country_psu`; this is analogous in purpose to the historical extension's `IDHSPSU` but constructed independently, neither derived from nor equal to it. DHS cluster GPS coordinates are randomly displaced for confidentiality (urban up to 2 km; rural 99% up to 5 km, 1% up to 10 km) [@Burgert2013] — inconsequential for the baseline models, which use no coordinates directly, but directly consequential for the historical extension's spatial matching (Section 3.5). Baseline models use the `v005`-derived weight, normalised within each estimation sample so each country contributes equal total weight mass regardless of raw sample size — an authorised pooling choice, not a claim about any country's raw size determining its influence (Section 4.5 gives the exact formula).

## 3.5 Historical extension: rounds, exposure source, and scope

The historical extension links an earlier DHS round for three countries, Ghana (2014), Kenya (2014), and Nigeria (2018), to IHME's annual gridded estimates of local access to improved drinking water (W_IMP) and improved sanitation (S_IMP), available for 2000–2017 [@LBDWaSH2020]. These product definitions were verified directly from the primary source during this project's development, not inferred from the 0–100 raster scale or file names alone. W_IMP is the sum of piped water (on- or off-premises) and other JMP-improved sources (protected wells and springs, bottled water, rainwater collection, bought water). S_IMP is the sum of sewer/septic sanitation and other JMP-improved facilities (improved and ventilated improved latrines, composting toilets). The source reports each as percent of population with access, on a continuous ~5×5 km surface. This project's own spatial extraction computes an **area average of the raster surface within each child's exposure buffer**, not the source study's own population-weighted administrative aggregate. This distinction is maintained throughout and restated wherever a historical coefficient is reported (Section 5.6; Section 6). Ethiopia's 2016 round is included only as a separately labelled, provisional sensitivity analysis (Appendix C), not as part of the established-date pooled sample. The reason is that the Ethiopian-calendar-to-Gregorian interview-date conversion could not be validated to the same standard as the three established-date rounds. The interview date does not place a child's exposure window — each window's months and exposure value are placed from the birth date alone (Section 3.7; Appendix C) — but it governs eligibility: date validity, the requirement that birth precede the interview, and, for the two postnatal windows, whether the window had completed by the interview date. Appendix C details the three candidate conversions and why one of them, labelled C1, is invalid and excluded from reporting. As stated in Section 1.2, this construction assigns each child a *modelled, area-level* local coverage estimate for a defined early-life window. It is a measurement improvement on the timing dimension specifically, not a record of what that child's own household facilities actually were, which neither this nor any other available data source can provide.

## 3.6 Historical spatial extraction: buffers, displacement, and coverage

Each child's exposure buffer is centred on its DHS cluster's (displaced) coordinates, using an azimuthal equidistant (AEQD) projection local to that cluster. **The primary buffer radius is 2 km for urban clusters and 5 km for rural clusters** (`scripts/historical_wash/10_spatial_extraction_v3.py`, `RADIUS_M`), stated explicitly here rather than left to be inferred from the DHS displacement distances. The displacement distances are a different matter: they describe the *uncertainty* in a cluster's true location, whereas the buffer radius defines the *area averaged* around its recorded location. A labelled sensitivity (Section 5.8, Appendix C.4) repeats the rural assignment at 10 km, and a separate sensitivity uses containing-pixel assignment instead of the buffer average. AEQD preserves true distance and direction from the cluster's own centre point exactly (relative error ≈6×10⁻¹²); it is not, in general, an equal-area projection, but at these small buffer radii its residual area distortion is negligible, confirmed directly. Within the buffer, the raster's value is averaged using a closed-form disk–polygon overlap computation (each pixel approximated by its corner-projected quadrilateral in the local AEQD plane), not a centroid, nearest-neighbour, or Monte Carlo approximation; this project's own validation found the approximation's own error small at the scales used (median relative pixel-area error ≈1.6×10⁻⁸; maximum mean-exposure difference 0.001 percentage points). Coverage completeness is checked explicitly: a child-year is assigned a valid extraction only when its valid-pixel area meets a documented tolerance (within 10⁻³ of full nominal coverage), and partial- or missing-coverage child-years are excluded rather than assigned a value from incomplete coverage; the reported mean is itself averaged only over valid pixels (confirmed to matter: averaging over the full nominal area instead would shift partial-coverage means by up to 73 percentage points).

## 3.7 Historical exposure windows and completion rules

Four candidate exposure windows are constructed for each child, applied to the same underlying annual raster series: the calendar year of birth; the nine months before birth (prenatal); the first 12 completed postnatal months (the working main window; Section 6.4 states why); and the first 24 postnatal months. Because IHME's product is annual, a window spanning two calendar years is assigned a weighted average of the relevant years' values (weighted by how many months fall in each), and every touched year must have a verified raster value. The interview-date completion check — excluding a child whose window had not yet elapsed by interview — is **not applied uniformly to all four windows**: it applies only to the two postnatal windows, where it is substantively necessary. The prenatal window needs no separate check, since it ends at birth and the interview date is always after birth by construction. The birth-year window has no completion check at all, so a child born partway through a year and interviewed before that year ends is still assigned the full annual value — a genuine, disclosed limit on what that window's eligibility rule enforces, not a concealed oversight.

A related but separate limitation concerns the annual resolution of the exposure surface itself: because IHME supplies one value per calendar year, the value used for a window's final touched year can reflect conditions from *after* a child's interview date even when the postnatal completion check is satisfied. This project's construction script flags every such case (a "post-interview-year-used" indicator, tallied and retained) but does not exclude or adjust on this basis — the flag is descriptive, not a filter — so this thesis does not claim every historical exposure value is strictly measured before the outcome it is matched to. The postnatal rule guarantees the *window's months* have elapsed by interview, not that the *annual value* representing the final year contains no post-interview information. Eligibility under any rule still requires a valid interview-date conversion, which is why Ethiopia's three candidates matter even for the birth-year window, whose exposure value does not itself depend on the precise interview date (Appendix C).

## 3.8 Historical covariates, weights, and the established-date measurement universe

The historical models control for cluster fixed effects, survey-qualified country-by-birth-year terms, child sex, maternal age, maternal education, and marital status, with the child's own completed age in months entered as an additional linear control. The country-by-birth-year terms are each country's own birth-year effects relative to that country's own earliest observed birth year, rather than one reference year shared across all three countries. Section 4.6 describes this convention and reports a dedicated diagnostic establishing that, once the cluster fixed effect and other covariates are accounted for, this specific choice does not itself change the fitted exposure coefficient, though it does change the raw design matrix. **The age control is constructed differently from the baseline's age variable, not identically.** The historical extension uses its own `age_m` field: reported completed months where delivered directly by the source recode (Ethiopia, Nigeria), and a documented calendar-month-difference construction otherwise (Ghana, Kenya). The baseline, by contrast, uses DHS's own standard `b19` field uniformly for all four countries, with no country-varying construction (Section 3.2). The two fields happen to serve an analogous purpose (a linear age-in-months control) but are built by different code over different samples, and neither is a copy of the other's rule. Household wealth quintile is **excluded from the historical extension's main specification**, for a documented reason that does not rest on fixed effects: current wealth may be a mediator or post-exposure outcome of the historical WASH exposure, and is correlated with the exposure itself, so including it risks conditioning on a variable downstream of the exposure of interest. **This is a genuine trade-off, not a one-sided argument for exclusion.** The competing concern is that current wealth, measured at the survey date, could instead (or also) proxy a household's *prior* socioeconomic circumstances during the child's early-life window — circumstances that could themselves confound the WASH–nutrition association, in which case omitting wealth leaves that confounding unaddressed rather than avoiding over-adjustment. Correlation between wealth and the exposure is not, on its own, a sufficient reason to omit a potential confounder; it is a reason to treat wealth's role as a substantive assumption requiring disclosure, not an automatic exclusion rule. Wealth is therefore included only as a labelled sensitivity check (Section 5.8), where the pooled water–HAZ coefficient is essentially unchanged (1.01 vs. 1.03) — showing the reported coefficient is not sensitive to this adjustment, without by itself adjudicating between the mediator and confounding readings. This reason is different from, and more defensible than, the claim that “cluster fixed effects absorb wealth”: that claim is false in general, since cluster fixed effects absorb only what is constant *within* a cluster, and wealth varies across households within the same cluster just as WASH status does, confirmed directly in this thesis's own baseline cluster-fixed-effects model, where wealth quintile *is* included and estimated as a standard covariate (Section 4.2).

Weighting uses PERWEIGHT, normalised so that each of the three established-date countries contributes an equal total observation weight within each model's own final estimation sample (not population-size weighting). This is the same authorised convention as the baseline, carried over for comparability, and it is reported alongside its unweighted counterpart for the headline cell (Section 5.6). The documented sampling weight for measured anthropometry specifically, as distinct from the general household/individual sampling weight, could not be located in official DHS documentation despite repeated attempts at alternative official sources (Appendix A records the exact sources tried and their outcomes). This project's working inference, stated explicitly wherever historical coefficients are reported, is that within-survey PERWEIGHT normalisation removes a constant measurement-subsample selection factor under an assumption of random household-level selection into the anthropometry subsample. It cannot and does not correct for any differential non-response among eligible children who were not measured. Historical results are therefore reported as **weighted conditional associations among eligible measured children**, not as population-representative estimates for any defined population, and this phrasing is used consistently rather than varied across sections.

## 3.9 A documented measurement-universe discrepancy: Nigeria 2018

Three distinct child populations arise for Nigeria 2018, and this thesis does not force them to a common count or treat the gap as resolved. The official 2018 NDHS report counts 12,806 (unweighted) children under five eligible and actually measured (men's-questionnaire subsample households, roughly a third of all households). The IPUMS-DHS birth-history extract used here contains 33,924 birth-history records (children born within approximately five years of the survey), of which 11,704 are non-missing for anthropometry. The final historical-extension estimation sample (water, HAZ, main post-12-month window) retains 6,912 after the window-completion, spatial-coverage, and singleton-cluster exclusions (Section 4.5). These differ by construction: the official count is eligible children in subsample households; the extract is birth-history children of interviewed women, which can omit children whose mothers are not in the sampled frame (raised in this project's documentation, not independently verified); the estimation sample further requires complete windows and spatial coverage. The gap between the official count and the extract (12,806 vs. 11,704) is disclosed as an open, unresolved question, not treated as resolved by any estimation choice here.
