# 2. Literature review and conceptual framework

## 2.1 From biological plausibility to demonstrated association

The hypothesised pathway from WASH to child growth runs through several distinct, separately evidenced mechanisms, not one undisputed mechanism: inadequate water and sanitation can increase exposure to faecal–oral pathogens, raising diarrhoeal disease, soil-transmitted helminth infection, and sub-clinical environmental enteric dysfunction (EED), each of which may redirect energy and nutrients away from linear growth, though the strength of evidence varies and EED specifically is the least settled [@CummingCairncross2016]. Even the strongest recent evidence against basic WASH interventions reducing stunting does not challenge this plausibility: the authors of the three major WASH trials discussed below state explicitly that "the biological plausibility of WASH as public health interventions is not challenged by these findings" [@Cumming2019, consensus message 2], arguing instead that the *specific, basic* interventions tested were insufficient to translate that mechanism into a detectable growth effect. Black et al. [-@Black2013] place WASH-related infection among the proximate determinants of child undernutrition in their widely cited *Lancet* framework, alongside diet and care practices, and Victora et al. [-@Victora2010] document that growth faltering concentrates from birth to roughly 24 months. This review addresses a narrower, empirical question: whether a *facility-type classification* — whether a household has an "improved" water source or sanitation facility in the standard WHO/UNICEF JMP sense used throughout this thesis — shows a detectable, policy-relevant statistical association with child growth once the obvious confounders are accounted for, distinct from whether WASH-related infection can plausibly affect growth at all.

## 2.2 Observational evidence and the confounding problem

A substantial observational literature finds WASH variables associated with child anthropometric outcomes in DHS and related survey data. Rakotomanana et al. [-@Rakotomanana2020] link the water and sanitation indicators of the WHO/UNICEF JMP to linear growth in children aged 6–23 months across several East African DHS surveys and find associations in the expected direction. Addae et al. [-@Addae2024], using Ghana's 2017/18 Multiple Indicator Cluster Survey (a national household survey comparable to, but distinct from, a DHS round), find household sanitation and socioeconomic status jointly associated with both stunting and wasting among children aged 6–23 months, and unprotected drinking water associated with wasting but not stunting. Günther and Fink [-@Gunther2010] pool 172 DHS surveys and estimate that improved water and sanitation are associated with lower child mortality, and Spears [-@Spears2013] documents that cross-country variation in average child height correlates with cross-country variation in open-defecation rates.

The central methodological difficulty in this literature, and one this thesis faces directly, is that household and community WASH status are not randomly assigned: both are strongly correlated with wealth, parental education, and local infrastructure, which independently predict child nutrition through channels unrelated to pathogen exposure. Headey and Palloni [-@HeadeyPalloni2019] address this directly, constructing a panel of 442 subnational regions across 59 countries from repeated DHS rounds and estimating difference-in-differences regressions that exploit within-region change over time, net of region fixed effects. Their results are mixed, not a single attenuation story: improved water access is statistically insignificantly associated with most outcomes (though piped water specifically predicts reduced stunting), and sanitation improvements predict sizeable reductions in diarrhoea and mortality but are *not* associated with stunting or wasting once the within-region design is used. This null sanitation–stunting association, once region-level time-invariant confounding is netted out, is a closer and more precise parallel to this thesis's own null household- and community-sanitation findings (Section 5) than a general "attenuation" story would suggest (Section 6 draws on it this way); it is not evidence that WASH associations "generally" weaken under better controls, which would overstate what this one study establishes.

## 2.3 Credible causal evidence: what the major trials found

The randomised evidence on WASH and child growth comes primarily from three cluster-randomised controlled trials, described here trial by trial and arm by arm because a loose paraphrase risks mischaracterising an exposure structure that genuinely differs from the cross-sectional literature of Sections 2.2 and 2.5, and attributing a finding from one arm to the wrong intervention. WASH-Benefits Bangladesh, WASH-Benefits Kenya, and the Sanitation Hygiene Infant Nutrition Efficacy (SHINE) trial in Zimbabwe each enrolled pregnant women and their children *in utero* and followed them prospectively for 18–24 months, with interventions assigned near the start of that window rather than at a survey date unrelated to each child's own age — an exposure-timing structure that differs materially from the DHS-based observational studies of Sections 2.2 and 2.5, and to which the exposure-timing limitation described in Section 1.2 for this thesis's own baseline design does not apply.

The three trials did not share one common factorial design. WASH-Benefits Bangladesh and Kenya each used a factorial design with **separate arms** for each component (water, sanitation, hygiene, combined-WASH, nutrition with no WASH component, and combined WASH-plus-nutrition), each against a control. SHINE used a simpler 2×2 design with **four arms only** (control, IYCF/nutrition alone, a single combined WASH package rather than separately randomised components, and combined IYCF-plus-WASH), so it never tested the three WASH components as independent arms the way the other two trials did. On linear growth, **every individual WASH-related arm in Bangladesh and Kenya showed no statistically detectable effect, and SHINE's single combined WASH arm likewise showed none** — the consensus paper's Figure 2 reflects this design difference directly, referring to "all WASH arms" (plural) for Bangladesh/Kenya and the "WASH arm" (singular) for Zimbabwe [@Cumming2019, pp. 2, 5 and Fig. 2]. The nutrition arm, containing no WASH component, showed a separate, modest positive growth effect described by the trial authors as a small gain despite high compliance [@Cumming2019, p. 6] — reported here so it is not misread as a WASH finding. The combined WASH-plus-nutrition arm showed **no additional growth benefit over nutrition alone** in any of the three trials [@Cumming2019, p. 2]. On diarrhoea, results were genuinely mixed: a substantial relative reduction in Bangladesh (against a low baseline), no detectable effect in Kenya, and none in Zimbabwe [@Cumming2019, pp. 2–5, Fig. 2].

"No statistically detectable effect" is the precise description used throughout, not "proven to be exactly zero": these are adequately powered trials with high internal validity, but a null result establishes an absence of detected effect at the trial's power, not certainty the true effect is zero, and the authors' own interpretation is explicit that the finding should not be generalised to *every possible* WASH intervention. The consensus statement attributes the null result to the limited scope of the specific interventions tested — household-level, "basic" WASH services (JMP ladder sense) that left community-level open defecation and broader water-supply infrastructure unchanged, rather than the "safely managed," networked services the authors argue may be needed for a detectable population-level effect — calling explicitly for research on more comprehensive, "transformative" WASH rather than concluding WASH cannot matter for growth [@Cumming2019]. This bears directly on how this thesis's own results should be read: its exposure, household- or community-level "improved" facility status, is likewise a facility-type classification rather than a comprehensive intervention package, so a null or weak association here is consistent with — though it does not confirm — the trial literature's finding, and equally consistent with confounding masking a real effect, measurement error, or this thesis's own limitations (Section 6); it is not read as evidence that WASH investment in general is ineffective.

A related quasi-experimental literature targets infant mortality, not growth, using a different identification strategy. Geruso and Spears [-@GerusoSpears2015] investigate why Muslim infants in India survive to their first birthday at a substantially higher rate than Hindu infants despite lower average wealth, consumption, and education: neighbourhoods with more Muslim households are *worse* on most observable dimensions, consistent with general socioeconomic disadvantage, except for open defecation, which Hindus practise at a rate roughly 25 points higher nationally (a religious difference unexplained by wealth). Because sanitation and general neighbourhood quality move in *opposite* directions across religious composition, the authors isolate a sanitation-specific channel accounting for a substantial share of the mortality advantage. This thesis cites the working-paper version for its identification strategy — a source of neighbourhood sanitation variation distinct from general local development, as an example of a design this thesis's own four-country, cross-sectional comparison cannot replicate — not as direct evidence about child growth, which the study does not examine.

## 2.4 Household versus community exposure

Most DHS-based observational studies reviewed here measure WASH at the household level only; a smaller, more recent strand examines community-level or spatially aggregated WASH exposure as a distinct construct. Donohue, Church, Assaf, and Mayala [-@Donohue2023] construct community-level improved-sanitation coverage (geospatially modelled, not a leave-one-out DHS-census rate) and relate it to stunting in Nigeria and Zambia (both 2018 DHS) via multilevel logistic regression, with a mixed, not general, result: in Nigeria, community coverage remained significantly, if modestly, associated with lower stunting odds after full adjustment (adjusted OR = 0.97, 95% CI [0.95, 0.99] per 10-point increase), while household sanitation lost significance in the same model; in Zambia, neither was significant at either stage. This motivates reporting household and community exposures separately, but is not evidence that community coverage generally carries explanatory relevance beyond household facilities — that would overstate a result significant in only one of two countries. Skoufias and Vinha [-@SkoufiasVinha2026], using DHS data from twenty Sub-Saharan African countries, similarly find **no significant externality from community-level water coverage**, but do find community sanitation significantly associated with lower fever/diarrhoea and higher height-for-age, varying by urban/rural area — though this contrasts with this thesis's own fully adjusted community HAZ models, which show no significant association for either measure (Section 5.2). Neither source's result confirms this thesis's specific coefficients, which come from a different sample, country set, and model. This thesis's leave-one-out community construction follows the same logic as these studies; the author is not aware of a study pairing this exact construction with a household-level comparison in the same four-country sample, though this is not a claim of an exhaustive search. Balk et al. [-@Balk2004], on child mortality in West Africa with spatially referenced survey data, provide an earlier precedent for treating DHS-cluster geography as a unit of analysis distinct from the household, applied to mortality rather than anthropometric outcomes.

A specific identification issue arises when community WASH is measured this way. The leave-one-out rate is a property of the cluster rather than of the individual child. Estimating it with cluster fixed effects, the natural approach for household WASH, would therefore remove nearly all of its substantive variation, identifying the coefficient almost entirely from the small mechanical residual that arises because a different household is excluded from each child's own calculation (Section 4 derives this precisely). This thesis therefore uses region, not cluster, fixed effects for the community specification.

## 2.5 Exposure timing in single-round, cross-sectional designs

The limitation introduced in Section 1.2 applies to studies that measure WASH status once, at a single survey date, and relate it to the anthropometric status of children of varying current ages within that round. This fits this thesis's own baseline design and the two anthropometric-outcome studies in Section 2.2, Rakotomanana et al. [-@Rakotomanana2020] and Addae et al. [-@Addae2024]. It does **not** describe Günther and Fink [-@Gunther2010] (mortality/morbidity outcomes, not anthropometric) or Spears [-@Spears2013] (a cross-country height/open-defecation correlation, not an individual child's outcome at varying current ages), nor the major WASH trials (Section 2.3, which assign exposure prospectively from near birth) or Headey and Palloni's [-@HeadeyPalloni2019] panel design (successive rounds within the same regions, not a single cross-section). Age controls and age-interaction terms, also estimated for this thesis's baseline sample (Sections 4–5), describe whether the *current* association differs with a child's age today; they do not reconstruct a child's own early-life environment, a different empirical question. This single-round limitation is why the historical extension attempts a different, early-life-window exposure assignment within its own sample (Section 1.2) — not a reason to expect comparing the two samples to isolate an exposure-timing effect (Section 6.2).

## 2.6 Geospatial exposure assignment and a matching-method precedent

Assigning a continuous, gridded exposure surface to a survey respondent's location requires handling two well-documented features of DHS data. First, coordinates are randomly displaced for confidentiality [@Burgert2013; @PerezHeydrich2013]: any extraction method reads pixels around the cluster's *recorded*, displaced coordinates, never its true, undisclosed location. Second, more than one method exists for reading a value from the surface: a containing-pixel extraction reads the single pixel the point falls in, while a buffer-based extraction averages pixels within a radius. This thesis uses an area-weighted buffer average as its primary method and containing-pixel extraction as a labelled sensitivity (Section 3.6; Section 5.8) — the two are not interchangeable, and the choice is made explicit here rather than left implicit. Johnson, Jacob, and Brown [-@JohnsonJacobBrown2013] provide an earlier precedent for linking satellite-derived environmental surfaces (forest cover) to DHS cluster locations for a different child-health outcome. Blom, Ortiz-Bobea, and Hoddinott [-@Blom2022] match historical, geocoded heat-exposure data to each child's own developmental window across repeated cross-sectional data from five West African countries, a design structure this thesis's historical extension borrows; their outcome is child nutritional status, which heat exposure (not WASH) is found to worsen. The study is cited only for this matching-method structure, not for any specific finding or as evidence about WASH, which it does not examine.

## 2.7 This thesis's contribution and the gap it addresses

This thesis combines two design elements not found combined elsewhere in this review: household and leave-one-out community WASH compared directly within the same sample and controls (Section 2.4); and, in a separate, bounded, explicitly preliminary extension, an attempt to assign modelled local WASH coverage to each child's own early-life window via geospatial matching (Section 2.6), within the three historical-sample countries. That this specific four-country combination is novel is not, on its own, evidence of a substantive contribution, and this thesis does not rely on country selection for that claim. The claimed methodological contribution is narrower: a direct, consistently controlled comparison of the two exposure levels, and a transparent, fully documented attempt — including its own unresolved issues — at an early-life exposure-matching design that neither Donohue et al. [-@Donohue2023] nor Skoufias and Vinha [-@SkoufiasVinha2026] implement, since both examine only current-round community exposure, not one matched to each child's developmental window; neither source is cited as calling for this extension, which is this thesis's own observation. The gap that remains open, and that this thesis does not claim to close, is the one Headey and Palloni [-@HeadeyPalloni2019] and the trial literature [@Cumming2019] already identify at a larger scale: credibly separating a WASH-specific effect from the broader package of local development that improved facilities tend to accompany. The historical extension narrows only the exposure-timing dimension of that problem within its own sample; it resolves neither the confounding dimension nor exposure-timing for the baseline sample, since the two samples differ in several other respects at once (Section 6.2).
